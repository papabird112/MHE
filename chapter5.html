A pneumatic system carries bulk solids inside a pipe, suspended in or pushed by moving air. Nothing else routes material around a plant so freely: a pipeline goes up, along, around a corner and through a wall, with no moving part between the feed point and the discharge. The price is energy — ten to thirty times what a bucket elevator spends on the same lift — and a design that is unforgiving about one number: the conveying velocity. This chapter is about getting that number right and then paying for it as little as possible.

5.1 What Pneumatic Conveying Is

In a pneumatic conveying system the carrier is a gas, almost always air, and the pipeline is the machine. Material is fed into a moving air stream at one end, travels as a suspension or as a series of plugs, and is separated from the air at the other end. There is no belt, no chain, no screw and no bucket: the only moving parts are the air mover, the feeder and the filter cleaning mechanism.

That is the whole appeal, and it is worth stating precisely. A pipeline can be routed like a service pipe — up a wall, across a roof, around an obstruction, through a fire barrier — and it is inherently dust-tight, because the material is inside a closed pipe at a pressure different from the room. One system can pick up from several points and deliver to several more, using diverter valves rather than duplicate machines. Against a belt or a chain conveyor, that flexibility is decisive in a congested plant.

The cost is energy. Air is a poor carrier: it has to move at 15 to 30 m/s to keep a granular material in suspension, and accelerating the solids, dragging them along the wall and then separating them again all cost work. Where a bucket elevator lifts a tonne 30 m for about 0.1 kWh (§4.20), a dilute-phase pneumatic system on a similar duty spends 1 to 4 kWh per tonne. The engineering question is never whether pneumatic conveying is efficient — it is not — but whether the flexibility is worth the bill.

The honest position on accuracy

Pneumatic conveying is the least exact design subject in this course. The gas-solid flow in a pipe resists closed-form treatment, so every practical method is semi-empirical, and published correlations for saltation velocity and solids friction carry uncertainties of 25 to 40 %. That does not make the calculations pointless — they size the equipment and tell you which variable matters — but for a new material, a long line or a critical duty, the profession's answer is a conveying trial on a test rig, and every sentence in this chapter should be read with that in mind.

Guess a pressure, work through, and compare. Two or three passes normally converge, and the worked example of §5.20 shows one.

Where this chapter sits

Chapters 2 to 4 moved material with a moving mechanical element. This chapter removes the mechanism from the conveying line altogether and puts the work into the air. The result is the most flexible and the most expensive of the continuous conveyors, and §1.19 is where the choice between them is actually settled.

Two practical rules come out of that table. If the plant has many pick-up points and one silo, think vacuum. If it has one silo and many destinations, think pressure. And if the dust is toxic or pharmaceutically active, vacuum has an argument no pressure system can answer: when a vacuum line leaks, air goes in.

5.4 Dilute and Dense Phase, and the State Diagram

Everything about a pneumatic system follows from one question: is the material carried in suspension, or pushed along the bottom of the pipe? The first is dilute phase, the second is dense phase, and they are two different machines that happen to share a pipe.

Read the diagram from right to left, which is the direction a designer thinks in. At high velocity the material is fully suspended and the pressure gradient is dominated by gas friction, which falls as velocity falls. Keep reducing the velocity and the gradient keeps falling — this is why running a line faster than necessary is simply wasted energy — until the point where particles start to settle. That is saltation. Past it the pipe partly fills with a moving layer, the free area shrinks, the gradient turns and climbs steeply, and shortly afterwards the line blocks.

Everything to the right of the saltation locus is dilute phase. Everything to the left of it can only be reached in a controlled way if the material and the pressure will support dense-phase flow (§5.16); for most materials that region is simply where lines plug.

5.5 Characterising the Material

In every earlier chapter the material properties that mattered were density, lump size, abrasiveness and flowability. Pneumatic conveying adds two that decide whether the system can work at all: how easily air passes through the bulk material, and how long the material holds air when it is disturbed.

The two rows in the middle are the ones that separate this chapter from the others. A permeable material like grain lets air pass through a stationary plug, so a plug can be pushed along by a pressure difference across it. An air-retaining material like a Group A powder stays fluidised for long enough to flow along the pipe bottom almost like a liquid. A material that is neither — poor permeability and poor air retention — can usually only be conveyed in dilute phase, and no amount of pressure will make dense phase behave.

5.6 Minimum Conveying Velocity and Saltation

The single most important number in a dilute-phase design is the minimum velocity at which the material stays in suspension. Below it the line blocks; far above it, energy, wear and product damage are all wasted. Practice therefore sets the design velocity just above the minimum, with a margin.

For a first estimate, use experience, expressed as a table of velocities by material class:

Table 5.8. Typical minimum conveying velocities, dilute phase, horizontal pipe.
MaterialMinimum velocity (m/s)Design velocity (m/s)
Fine cohesive powders: cement, flour, fly ash8–1211–15
Fine granular: sugar, salt, fine sand12–1615–20
Grain: wheat, maize, soybean meal14–1818–23
Plastic pellets and granules16–2020–25
Coarse or heavy granular: gravel, ore fines18–2523–30
Vertical lift, any materialAdd 2–3 m/s to the horizontal figure—

For a calculated estimate, the standard correlation is Rizk's, which relates the saltation velocity to the solids loading ratio, the particle size and the pipe bore:

μ = 110α Usalt√(gD)β  with α = 1440dp + 1.96 and β = 1100dp + 2.5, dp in m (5.1)
Usalt = √(gD) · (μ · 10α)1/β (5.2)

and the design velocity follows with a margin:

Udesign = (1.2 to 1.5) Usalt  at the point of lowest pressure — usually the feed point (5.3)

Two cautions. First, Eq. 5.1 is a correlation fitted to particular test data; treat its answer as an estimate with the 25 to 40 % uncertainty of §5.1, and cross-check it against Table 5.8. Second, the velocity to design on is the one at the beginning of the line, because that is where the air is densest and slowest; everywhere downstream is faster (§5.8).

A worked check of Eq. 5.2

Polyethylene pellets, dp = 3 mm, in a 100 mm pipe at μ = 10: α = 1440(0.003) + 1.96 = 6.28, β = 1100(0.003) + 2.5 = 5.80, √(gD) = √0.981 = 0.99. So Usalt = 0.99 × (10 × 106.28)1/5.80 = 0.99 × 18.0 = 17.8 m/s, and the design velocity is about 21 m/s — which is what Table 5.8 says for plastic pellets. When a correlation and experience agree, you may proceed; when they do not, find out why before ordering the blower.

5.7 Solids Loading Ratio

The solids loading ratio, also called phase density, is the mass of solids conveyed per unit mass of air. It is dimensionless, it is the first number an experienced engineer asks for, and it tells you immediately which regime you are in:

μ = ṁsṁg  kg of solids per kg of air (5.4)
Table 5.9. Reading the solids loading ratio.
μRegimeComment
2–5Very diluteShort lines, low tonnage, or a velocity chosen far above what the material needs. Energy per tonne is poor
5–15Dilute phaseThe normal operating band of a blower-based system
15–25Medium phaseA transition region; possible with permeable or air-retentive material at moderate pressure
25–100Dense phaseBlow tank territory, 1–3 bar, low velocity
over 100Extruded or plug flowVery short lines, high pressure, or fluidised dense phase in Group A powders

The ratio also explains the economics of the chapter. Energy per tonne conveyed falls as μ rises, because you are paying the compressor for less air per tonne of product. Every design improvement in pneumatic conveying — stepping the pipeline, using dense phase, lowering the velocity to just above saltation — is in the end an attempt to raise μ without blocking the line.

5.8 Gas Expansion Along the Pipeline

Air is compressible, and in a pneumatic line the pressure changes by a factor of one and a half or more from end to end. Because the mass flow of air is constant along the pipe, the velocity must rise as the pressure falls. This one fact causes most of the surprises in pneumatic conveying.

ρg = pRT  with R = 287 J/(kg·K) for air, p absolute (5.5)
U = ṁgρgA      U2 = U1 p1p2  isothermal, constant bore (5.6)

The consequence is worth stating twice. The velocity that must not be too low occurs at the feed point, and the velocity that must not be too high occurs at the discharge. In the worked example of §5.20 the air enters at 20 m/s and leaves at 32 m/s: the same air stream that is barely able to pick the pellets up at one end is fast enough to be chipping them at the other. Stepping the pipeline (§5.11) is the standard answer.

5.9 Pressure Drop: The Components

The total pressure drop of a dilute-phase line is the sum of six contributions. Each one is a separate physical effect, and knowing which one dominates tells you what to change.

Δpgas,fric = λ LD ρmUm22  λ ≈ 0.02 for commercial steel pipe in turbulent flow (5.7)
Δpsol,fric = λs μ LD ρmUm22  λs = 0.002–0.008, from Table 5.10 (5.8)
Δpacc,sol = ṁsUsA      Δpacc,gas = ρmUm22  Us ≈ 0.8U for granular material (5.9)
Δplift,sol = ṁsgHAUs      Δplift,gas = ρmgH  H = net vertical rise (5.10)
Δptotal = (Δpgas,fric + Δpsol,fric + Δpacc + Δplift + Δpfeeder + Δpfilter) · k  k = 1.15–1.25 design margin (5.11)

The mean values ρm and Um are evaluated at the mean pressure along the line, which for a first pass is the arithmetic mean of the two ends. Because that mean depends on the pressure drop being calculated, the whole thing iterates, as §5.2 warned.

Which term dominates?

In the worked example of §5.20, of a 0.368 bar pipeline loss the solids friction is 47 %, the gas friction 25 %, the solids acceleration 19 % and the lift only 7 %. That ranking is typical of a long horizontal dilute-phase line, and it says where to look for savings: the solids friction term carries μ and U2, so reducing the velocity to just above saltation is worth more than anything else you can do to the pipe.

5.10 Bends and Equivalent Length

A bend costs far more than its own length. The solids are slowed by the impact and by sliding round the outside of the bend, and then have to be re-accelerated in the straight pipe after it — and that re-acceleration, not the turn itself, is most of the loss. The standard treatment is to replace each bend with an equivalent length of straight horizontal pipe:

Leq = Lhoriz + Lvert + Σ Lbend  then use Leq in Eqs. 5.7 and 5.8 (5.12)

Two design rules follow. Use as few bends as the route allows, because in the worked example three bends account for 24 m of the 99 m equivalent length — a quarter of the friction loss for 3 m of actual pipe. And never put a bend immediately after the feed point or immediately before a second bend: the solids need 5 to 10 m of straight pipe to get back up to speed, and two bends in quick succession give a much larger loss than the table suggests.

5.11 Pipeline Sizing and the Stepped Pipeline

The bore follows from the air mass flow and the velocity required at the feed point. Because standard pipe sizes are discrete, the practical method is to choose a bore, compute the resulting velocity and loading ratio, and check both.

Then comes the expansion problem of §5.8. If the inlet velocity is set just above saltation and the pressure ratio is 1.6, the outlet velocity is 1.6 times the inlet — often well into the region where pellets chip, sugar caramelises on the bend and the bend itself wears out. The remedy is to step the pipeline up one size at one or two points along the route:

A2A1 = Ubefore stepUafter step  and Uafter step must stay above Usalt (5.13)

The second half of that equation is the trap. A step that is too generous drops the velocity below the minimum conveying velocity and blocks the line at the step — which is why a stepped pipeline is designed with the saltation calculation repeated for the new bore, not by eye.

A step that would have blocked the line

In the worked example the velocity reaches 28 m/s when the pressure has fallen to 1.14 bar abs. Stepping from 100 mm to 125 mm there would cut the velocity by (100/125)2 = 0.64, to 17.9 m/s — below the 20 m/s those pellets need. Stepping to 110 mm instead gives 0.826 × 28 = 23.1 m/s, comfortably above the minimum and comfortably below the damage limit. One size up, not two.

5.12 Air Movers

The air mover is selected on the pressure it must develop and the volume it must deliver — and the two are usually quoted at different conditions, which is a standing source of error.

For the power, the isothermal expression is accurate enough for a teaching calculation and conservative in the right direction:

P = ṁgRT ln(p2/p1)η  η = 0.55–0.70 for a blower package including drive (5.14)

Three practical points. A positive-displacement blower delivers a nearly constant volume regardless of pressure, so its mass flow changes with inlet conditions — hot weather reduces the air it delivers, which reduces the conveying velocity, which is why some systems block on the hottest afternoon of the year. A blower needs a relief valve, because it will happily generate enough pressure to damage itself against a blocked line. And the discharge air is hot: 50 to 90 °C above ambient at 0.8 bar, which matters for plastics, sugar and anything that softens.

5.14 Air Leakage Through the Feeder

A rotary valve seals by clearance, not by contact, so at every revolution each empty pocket carries compressed air back from the line into the hopper. That leaked air has to be supplied by the blower on top of the conveying air, and it has to escape from the hopper somewhere — usually through a vent filter, which is why a hopper above a rotary valve almost always has one.

Table 5.14. Indicative rotary valve air leakage.

150 mm0.5–0.9 m³/min0.8–1.4 m³/min
200 mm0.8–1.4 m³/min1.3–2.2 m³/min
250 mm1.2–2.0 m³/min1.9–3.2 m³/min
300 mm1.7–2.8 m³/min2.7–4.5 m³/min

The numbers are worth putting in proportion. The worked example needs 15.1 m³/min of conveying air; a 250 mm valve at 0.6 bar leaks roughly 1.5 m³/min, about 10 %, and the blower must be sized for 16.6 m³/min. Ignore the leakage and the system runs 10 % slow, which on a line designed with a 20 % velocity margin eats half the margin before anything has worn.

Worn valves leak far more. A valve whose rotor tips have worn 1 mm can pass two or three times its original leakage, and the first symptom is a conveying line that has become unreliable "for no reason". Rotor clearance is a scheduled measurement, not a repair.

5.15 Separation and Filtration

At the end of the line the solids must be taken out of the air, and the air must be clean enough to release or recirculate. Two devices do the work, usually in series.

  • Cyclone. Centrifugal separation, no moving parts, low maintenance, tolerant of high loading and high temperature. It removes most of the mass but its efficiency falls off sharply below about 10 µm, so it is a pre-separator rather than a final filter.
  • Fabric filter. Bags or cartridges with reverse-pulse cleaning, which is the normal final stage. Sized on the air-to-cloth ratio: the volume flow of air divided by the cloth area.
Af = Qvf  Q in m³/s at filter conditions, vf the air-to-cloth ratio in m/s (5.15)

A filter is therefore part of the conveying calculation, not an accessory bolted on afterwards — which is why §5.9 carries a filter term in Eq. 5.11. Allow 0.02 to 0.05 bar for a clean filter and check what the supplier's curve says at the dirty-filter condition.

5.16 Dense Phase and Plug Conveying

Dense phase is not simply dilute phase run slowly. It is a different flow regime, it needs a different feeder and a different pressure, and — crucially — it only works for materials whose properties allow it.

Table 5.17. The three dense-phase mechanisms, and what each needs from the material.
MechanismHow it movesMaterial requirementTypical products
Fluidised dense phaseThe material stays aerated and flows along the pipe bottom almost as a liquid Good air retention: Geldart group A, and fine group C Cement, fly ash, alumina, fine catalyst, flour
Plug or slug flowDiscrete plugs are pushed along, each with a pressure difference across it High permeability so air can pass through a plug: group D Pellets, grain, granulated sugar, coarse salt
Assisted plug flowA bypass pipe or air-injection valves break long plugs before they lock Materials that are neither permeable nor air-retentive Granules with wide size distribution; many food powders

The failure mode of plug flow is worth understanding, because it is the reason bypass lines exist. As a plug grows longer, the pressure needed to push it rises; if it grows long enough, the pressure required exceeds what the system can provide and the plug locks solid. A bypass pipe — a small-bore internal or external line with ports along the main pipe — lets air short-circuit past a growing plug and break it into shorter ones. An air-knife or booster arrangement does the same by injecting air at intervals along the line.

What dense phase buys is velocity, and therefore everything velocity causes: at 3 m/s instead of 25 m/s, product degradation and bend wear largely disappear, the air volume per tonne falls by a factor of three or four, and the filter gets much smaller. What it costs is pressure — a compressor rather than a blower, thicker-walled pipe, a pressure vessel as the feeder — and batch rather than continuous operation in most designs.

When the answer is “test it”

Dense-phase conveyability cannot be read off a table. A permeability test and a de-aeration test narrow the field, but the decision on a new material is made on a test rig with the real product, the real distance and the real bends, because the same powder can convey beautifully at 2 bar and 40 m and refuse to move at 2 bar and 80 m. The honest design answer, when asked for dense phase on an unfamiliar material, is a conveying trial — and saying so is better engineering than a confident calculation.

5.17 Wear and Product Degradation

Velocity causes both problems, and both scale far more steeply than linearly with it. Particle kinetic energy goes as U2, and erosive wear of a bend by hard particles is commonly found to scale as U2.5 to U4. Doubling the velocity can therefore multiply bend wear by ten or more:

wear rate2wear rate1 = (U2/U1)n  n = 2.5–4 for erosive wear; n ≈ 2–3 for particle breakage (5.16)
Table 5.18. Controlling wear and degradation.

Blind-tee bendsThe trapped material takes the impact; very long life on abrasive dutyThe highest pressure loss of any bend; unsuitable for sticky or perishable product

Smooth-bore pipe, well-aligned jointsFewer local impacts; less streaming wearInstallation discipline
Stainless or polished pipe for food dutyPrevents product build-up and contaminationCost

The degradation question has a measurable answer: convey a sample and sieve it. For plastic pellets the symptom is "angel hair" and fines; for sugar it is dust and caramelised deposits on bends; for coated fertiliser it is loss of coating and caking downstream. If the product specification includes a fines limit, the conveying velocity is part of the quality system, not just the engineering.

5.18 Static Electricity and Explosion Risk

A pneumatic line is a closed pipe carrying a dust cloud in air at high velocity, with millions of particle-wall contacts per second. If the dust is combustible, four of the five conditions of §4.18 are present continuously, and the fifth — ignition — is being manufactured by the flow itself in the form of static charge.

Table 5.19. Ignition sources in a pneumatic system, and their controls.
SourceMechanismControl
Electrostatic charging of the solidsParticle-wall contact charges both; the charge accumulates on anything not earthed Metal pipe, every section bonded and earthed, conductive hoses only, resistance checked on a schedule
Insulated conductor in the systemAn unbonded flange, a plastic sight glass ring, an ungrounded filter cage builds a spark-capable charge Bond everything; specify conductive or antistatic non-metal parts; test continuity after any maintenance
Tramp metalA steel object entering the line makes sparks at a bendMagnet or metal detector upstream of the feeder
Overheated blower or bearingHot surfaces above the dust's ignition temperatureTemperature monitoring, relief valve on the blower, filter on the blower inlet
Smouldering nest from previous operationProduct left in a dead leg self-heatsAvoid dead legs; clean-out and inspection; spark detection on high-risk duty
Mechanical friction in a plugged lineA rotary valve grinding against packed materialBlockage detection and interlocked shutdown

Protection beyond prevention follows the same logic as for a bucket elevator: explosion venting on the receiver and filter, flame isolation between the line and the silo, suppression where venting is impossible, and — uniquely available to pneumatic systems — inerting. Because the system is closed and the gas is already being handled, running it on nitrogen in a closed loop removes the oxygen leg of the pentagon altogether. That is the standard answer for aluminium powder, sulphur and many pharmaceutical products, and CEN/TR 15281 is the guidance document.

5.19 Commissioning and Troubleshooting

A pneumatic system is commissioned with instruments, not with opinions. Two measurements tell you almost everything: the pressure at the feed point, and the delivered rate.

1Run on air alone and record the pressure at the feed pointThe air-only line loss; compare with Eq. 5.7. A large discrepancy means the line is not what the drawing says
2Check the air volume against the blower curve at the measured pressureWhether the air mover is delivering, and whether the velocity is what the design assumed
3Feed at a low rate and step the rate up, recording pressure at each stepThe operating line on the state diagram of Figure 5.3; where the pressure starts to climb steeply, you are approaching the minimum velocity
4Weigh the delivered material over a timed periodThe actual rate, and with the air flow, the actual loading ratio
5Sieve a sample from the receiver against a sample from the feed hopperThe degradation the velocity is causing

Clearing a blocked line, safely

A blocked conveying line is a pressure vessel full of compacted solids. Do not open a coupling to “have a look”: isolate the air, vent the line through a designed vent point, confirm zero pressure on a gauge, and only then break a joint — starting at the end nearest the blockage, and never standing in line with the opening. The compressed air behind a plug has enough stored energy to fire the plug out like a projectile.

5.20 Worked Design Example

Polyethylene pellets are to be conveyed from a rail-car unloading hopper to a silo: 60 m horizontal, 15 m vertical, three long-radius bends, 10 t/h. Size a dilute-phase positive-pressure system.

Table 5.23. Design data.
MaterialPolyethylene pellets, dp ≈ 3.5 mm, free flowing, friable at high velocity
Particle / bulk density920 / 550 kg/m³
Required rate ṁs10 t/h = 2.778 kg/s
Route60 m horizontal + 15 m vertical + 3 long-radius bends
Air temperature20 °C at the blower inlet; discharge to atmosphere at 1.0 bar abs
Feeder250 mm rotary valve under the hopper

Step 1 — Phase, system type and design velocity

Pellets are Geldart group D: permeable, no air retention. Dense phase would be possible only as plug flow with a bypass, and the duty is modest and continuous, so choose dilute phase, positive pressure, rotary valve feed.

Table 5.8 gives 16–20 m/s minimum for plastic pellets, and Eq. 5.2 with dp = 3 mm, D = 100 mm and μ = 10 gave 17.8 m/s in §5.6. Take the design velocity at the feed point as 20 m/s.

Dilute phase · positive pressure · U1 = 20 m/s

Step 2 — First guess at the pressure, and the air mass flow

Guess a line pressure drop of 0.6 bar, so the feed point is at p1 = 1.6 bar abs and the receiver at p2 = 1.0 bar abs. From Eq. 5.5, ρ1 = 160,000/(287 × 293) = 1.903 kg/m³ and ρ2 = 1.189 kg/m³.

Take a 100 mm bore: A = 0.007854 m². Then ṁg = ρ1AU1 = 1.903 × 0.007854 × 20 = 0.2989 kg/s.

ṁg = 0.299 kg/s in a 100 mm line

Step 3 — Solids loading ratio and the exit velocity

μ = 2.778/0.2989 = 9.3, which Table 5.9 puts squarely in the dilute-phase band — consistent with the assumption of step 1.

From Eq. 5.6 the exit velocity is U2 = 20 × 1.6/1.0 = 32 m/s. That is high for pellets, and step 8 deals with it.

μ = 9.3 · U2 = 32 m/s

Step 4 — Equivalent length and mean conditions

Three long-radius bends at 8 m each (Table 5.11): Leq = 60 + 15 + 24 = 99 m.

Mean density ρm = (1.903 + 1.189)/2 = 1.546 kg/m³, so the mean velocity is Um = 0.2989/(1.546 × 0.007854) = 24.6 m/s and the dynamic pressure is ρmUm2/2 = 468 Pa.

Leq = 99 m · Um = 24.6 m/s

Step 5 — Pressure drop, term by term

With L/D = 990, λ = 0.02, λs = 0.004 (Table 5.10) and Us = 0.8Um = 19.7 m/s:

Table 5.24. Pressure drop by component.

Add 0.05 bar for the rotary valve and 0.05 bar for the filter, then a 1.2 design margin (Eq. 5.11): (0.368 + 0.10) × 1.2 = 0.56 bar. The guess was 0.6 bar, so the calculation has converged on the first pass and no second iteration is needed.

Δptotal ≈ 0.56 bar, against 0.6 assumed — converged

Step 6 — Air mover

Volume at the blower inlet: Q = ṁg/ρatm = 0.2989/1.189 = 0.251 m³/s = 15.1 m³/min. Add the rotary valve leakage of about 1.5 m³/min from Table 5.14, giving 16.6 m³/min at 0.6 bar g — squarely positive-displacement blower territory (Table 5.12).

Power from Eq. 5.14: P = 0.2989 × 287 × 293 × ln(1.6) = 11.8 kW isothermal; at η = 0.65 that is 18.2 kW, so select a 22 kW package.

PD blower, 16.6 m³/min at 0.6 bar g, 22 kW motor

Step 7 — Filter

Air at the receiver: Q2 = 0.2989/1.189 = 0.251 m³/s. Pellets generate coarse dust, so take vf = 0.025 m/s (Table 5.15): Af = 0.251/0.025 = 10.1 m² of cloth.

Reverse-pulse filter, 10 m² cloth, on the silo

Step 8 — Stepping the pipeline

The exit velocity of 32 m/s will chip pellets and produce angel hair. Take 28 m/s as the limit: from Eq. 5.6 the velocity reaches 28 m/s when the pressure has fallen to 20 × 1.6/28 = 1.14 bar abs, roughly three-quarters of the way along the route.

Step there. Going to 125 mm would cut the velocity to (100/125)2 × 28 = 17.9 m/s, below the 20 m/s the pellets need — the line would block at the step. Going to 110 mm gives 0.826 × 28 = 23.1 m/s, above the minimum and below the damage limit.

Step 100 mm → 110 mm at about 1.14 bar abs

Step 9 — Protection and checks

Polyethylene dust is combustible, so the system is earthed and bonded throughout with conductive hose only, a magnet is fitted upstream of the rotary valve, the filter and receiver carry explosion venting sized from the dust's test data, and the blower has a relief valve and discharge temperature monitoring (§5.18).

Interlocks: air established before the feeder starts; feeder stops on low air flow, high line pressure or high filter differential.

Earthing, magnet, venting, relief valve, air-before-feeder interlock

5.22 Problems

Before you start

Try the 40-question self-check first. It covers the same ground in multiple-choice form with worked explanations, and it will show you which sections to re-read before you spend time on these.

  1. Safety, first. A conveying line has blocked. An operator proposes to break the coupling nearest the blockage while the blower keeps running, “so the air helps push the plug out”. State what will physically happen, and write the correct four-step procedure.
  2. Safety, second. Using the five conditions of §4.18, explain why a pneumatic line carrying a combustible powder generates its own ignition source, and state the one protective measure available to a pneumatic system that a bucket elevator cannot use.
  3. A system conveys 8 t/h of solids with an air mass flow of 0.25 kg/s. Find the solids loading ratio and state the regime. [Answer: μ = 8.9 — dilute phase]
  4. A 150 mm line carries air at 18 m/s, 1.4 bar abs and 20 °C. Find the air density and the air mass flow. [Answer: ρ = 1.665 kg/m³, A = 0.01767 m², ṁg = 0.530 kg/s]
  5. The line in Problem 4 discharges to atmosphere at 1.0 bar abs. What is the exit velocity, and why does it matter? [Answer: 25.2 m/s; the discharge end is where wear and product degradation occur]
  6. Estimate the saltation velocity for a 2 mm granular material in an 80 mm pipe at a loading ratio of 12, using Eq. 5.2. [Answer: α = 4.84, β = 4.70, √(gD) = 0.886, Usalt = 16.1 m/s, so design at about 20 m/s]
  7. A 100 mm line is 80 m long with mean air density 1.4 kg/m³ and mean velocity 25 m/s. Find the gas friction pressure drop with λ = 0.02. [Answer: dynamic pressure 437.5 Pa, L/D = 800, Δp = 7,000 Pa]
  8. For the same line at μ = 10 and λs = 0.005, find the solids friction pressure drop, and comment on the ratio to the gas friction term. [Answer: 17,500 Pa — two and a half times the gas friction, which is the normal picture]
  9. A vertical section lifts 3 kg/s of solids 20 m in a 100 mm pipe with a particle velocity of 20 m/s. Find the lift pressure drop for the solids. [Answer: 3,750 Pa]
  10. A blower must deliver 0.30 kg/s of air from 1.0 to 1.8 bar abs at 20 °C with a package efficiency of 0.65. Find the power. [Answer: 22.8 kW]
  11. If the system in Problem 10 conveys 12 t/h, what is the energy per tonne? Compare it with the 0.109 kWh/t of the bucket elevator in §4.20 and comment. [Answer: 1.90 kWh/t, about 17 times the bucket elevator]
  12. A receiver handles 0.26 m³/s of air carrying a fine powder. Size the filter cloth area at an air-to-cloth ratio of 0.02 m/s, and say what happens if you install half that area. [Answer: 13 m²; at half the area the differential pressure rises, the pressure available for conveying falls, the velocity falls and the line eventually blocks]
  13. A 250 mm rotary valve feeds a line at 0.6 bar g. The conveying air requirement is 15 m³/min. Using Table 5.14, estimate the valve leakage and the blower duty, and state what happens to the conveying velocity if the leakage is ignored. [Answer: about 1.5 m³/min, so the blower needs 16.5 m³/min; ignoring it leaves the line running about 10 % slow, eating half a 20 % velocity margin]
  14. A line runs at 18 m/s at the feed point, where the pressure is 1.5 bar abs. The material must not exceed 26 m/s. At what pressure must the pipeline be stepped, and what step size is acceptable if the minimum conveying velocity is 17 m/s? [Answer: the limit is reached at 18 × 1.5/26 = 1.04 bar abs; a step from 100 to 110 mm gives 0.826 × 26 = 21.5 m/s, acceptable, while 125 mm would give 16.6 m/s, below the minimum]
  15. A plant conveys cement 120 m in dilute phase at 14 m/s and wants to halve the energy per tonne. Explain which changes could achieve that, in order of effectiveness, and what each one costs.
  16. Two powders are offered for the same pneumatic system: one is Geldart group A with good air retention, the other group B with poor air retention but high permeability. State which dense-phase mechanism suits each, and what happens if you apply the wrong one.
  17. A bend wears through in eight weeks at 24 m/s. Estimate the life at 18 m/s if the wear exponent is 3, and say why the answer should still be treated as an estimate. [Answer: (24/18)3 = 2.37, so roughly 19 weeks; the exponent itself is uncertain between 2.5 and 4, and wear depends on the impact angle and the bend material as well]
  18. A pharmaceutical plant must move a toxic, combustible powder from three reactors to one packing line. Recommend a system type and justify it on containment, explosion protection and layout, referring to Table 5.4.
  19. A system that has run correctly for two years starts blocking on hot afternoons. Give the likely cause in terms of the blower characteristic and the gas law, and two ways to fix it.
  20. Design problem. Wheat flour (ρbulk = 550 kg/m³, Geldart group C, combustible dust) must be conveyed at 6 t/h from a bag-dump station to a mixer 40 m away horizontally and 10 m up, with two bends, using a vacuum system with a receiver and filter over the mixer. Work through steps 1 to 7 of Table 5.3: choose the phase and velocity from Table 5.8, select a bore, find ṁg and μ at the mean vacuum condition, estimate the pressure drop, size the exhauster and the filter, and list the explosion protection measures the dust requires. [Guide: at a mean pressure of 0.6 bar abs the air density is 0.714 kg/m³; a 125 mm bore at 14 m/s gives ṁg = 0.123 kg/s and μ = 13.6, which is at the top of the dilute band and acceptable for a fine powder]
