1.1 Materials Handling: Scope and Principles

Materials handling is the movement, storage, protection and control of materials throughout manufacturing, warehousing, distribution, consumption and disposal. Note what the definition does not say: it does not say that handling changes the product. Handling adds cost, time and risk of damage, but no value. In a typical process plant it consumes 20–50 % of operating cost and is responsible for a disproportionate share of lost-time injuries.

The design objective follows directly: move the required tonnage at the lowest total cost per tonne, with acceptable risk to people and product. Everything in this course is a means to that end.

The ten principles of materials handling

The College-Industry Council on Material Handling Education states ten principles. They are not equations, but they are the checklist a design review will hold you to.

Table 1.1. The ten principles and their practical test.
#PrincipleWhat it means in practice
1PlanningDefine the method, the material, the move and the required performance before selecting hardware.
2StandardisationFewer equipment types and fewer spare part numbers; commonality across the plant.
3WorkMinimise handling work = flow × distance. The cheapest move is the one you eliminate.
4ErgonomicFit the task to the person; eliminate repetitive manual lifting.
5Unit loadHandle many items as one load (pallet, container, bulk stream) rather than individually.
6Space utilisationUse the cube, not just the floor. Storage height is usually the cheapest capacity.
7SystemHandling, storage, production and transport are one system; local optima do not add up.
8AutomationAutomate where volume, repetition and consistency justify it — not by default.
9EnvironmentalEnergy use, dust, noise, spillage and end-of-life disposal are design inputs.
10Life-cycle costJudge on capital + energy + maintenance + downtime over the whole life, not purchase price.
Why this matters for Chapter 1

Principles 3, 5 and 10 explain why belt conveyors dominate bulk handling. A belt conveys a continuous "unit load", the path is fixed and short, and its energy consumption per tonne-kilometre is roughly an order of magnitude below that of trucking the same material.

Bulk versus unit load

The single most important early decision is whether the material behaves as a bulk solid (a divisible stream characterised statistically: coal, grain, ore, cement) or as a unit load (a discrete, countable item: cartons, pallets, coils). Bulk material is handled by cross-section and mass flow rate; unit loads are handled by count and cycle time. Belt conveyors serve both, but the design methods differ, and this chapter treats the bulk case.

1.3 The Belt Conveyor

A belt conveyor carries material on the upper surface of an endless flexible belt that runs over a series of idler rollers between two terminal pulleys. One pulley is driven; friction between that pulley and the belt transmits the whole of the tractive force. The belt is both the carrying element and the tension member — a distinction from chain conveyors, where those functions are separated.

Where the belt conveyor wins — and where it does not

AdvantagesLimitations
Very low energy per tonne-kilometre; a large fraction of the power goes into useful lift. Fixed path only. Any change of direction needs a transfer point or a curved special design.
Very high capacity — single flights exceed 10 000 t/h. Inclination limited to roughly 18–20° for smooth belts.
Long single flights (kilometres) with one drive. High capital cost per metre; not economic for low utilisation.
Gentle on friable material; low degradation. Belt is the most expensive component and is vulnerable to impact, tramp metal, heat and oil.
Few moving parts, low maintenance per tonne, easy to automate and weigh. Open belt: spillage, dust and carryback need active management.

1.4 Principal Components

The belt

The belt performs three jobs at once: it carries the load, it transmits the tension, and it forms the trough that contains the material. It is normally 30–50 % of the capital cost of the conveyor, so most of the design effort is really about protecting the belt.

Idlers

Idlers support the belt and shape the trough. The main types are:

  • Troughing (carrying) idlers — usually three equal rolls; the outer rolls are inclined at the trough angle λ (20°, 30°, 35° or 45°). Five-roll sets are used on very wide, high-capacity belts.
  • Impact idlers — rubber-ringed rolls at close spacing (0.3–0.5 m) in the loading zone, to absorb the energy of falling material and protect the belt and splices.
  • Return idlers — single flat rolls, or two rolls in a shallow V, at 2.5–3.5 m spacing. Rubber-disc returns are used where carryback is a problem.
  • Training (self-aligning) idlers — pivoting sets that steer a wandering belt back to centre; typically every 10th carrying set and every 3rd return set.
  • Transition idlers — progressively reduced trough angle between the last full trough set and the terminal pulley, to limit belt edge stress.

Pulleys

  • Drive (head) pulley — transmits the tractive force; almost always rubber- or ceramic-lagged, often with a herringbone or diamond groove pattern to shed water.
  • Tail pulley — return the belt at the loading end; often wing-type or spiral to reject trapped material.
  • Snub pulley — small pulley that increases the wrap angle on the drive pulley, typically from 180° to 210–240°.
  • Bend pulleys — route the belt through the take-up.
  • Take-up pulley — moveable pulley that maintains tension.

Drive unit

Squirrel-cage motor, flexible coupling, gear reducer and a low-speed coupling to the drive shaft. Two additions matter for inclined conveyors:

  • A backstop (anti-runback), sized to hold the fully loaded incline. Without it, a power failure on a loaded uphill conveyor lets the belt run backwards and pile material at the tail.
  • Controlled starting — fluid coupling, soft starter or variable frequency drive — to limit starting tension to typically 1.3–1.5 times the running tension.

Loading, discharge and cleaning

  • Loading chute and skirtboards — deliver material centrally, in the direction of belt travel, and as close to belt speed as possible. Skirtboards contain the material until it has accelerated and settled.
  • Discharge — over the head pulley into a chute; or intermediately by tripper (a movable two-pulley assembly that lifts the belt) or by plough (a blade that sweeps material off, only for flat belts).
  • Belt cleaners — a primary scraper on the head pulley face and a secondary scraper just beyond it; a V-plough on the return run ahead of the tail pulley keeps spillage out of the tail. Carryback is the single largest cause of conveyor housekeeping cost.

A conveyor belt has a carcass, which carries the tension, and covers, which protect the carcass and provide the friction surface.

Covers

Cover thickness is quoted as top + bottom, e.g. 4 + 2 mm. The top cover is thicker because it takes the loading impact and abrasion; a common rule is that the top cover should be at least twice the bottom. Cover grades to ISO 10247 / DIN 22102 include:

  • Grade W / X — high abrasion resistance, general bulk duty.
  • Grade Y — general purpose.
  • Grade Z — low duty, non-abrasive material.
  • Heat resistant (T-grades), oil resistant, flame retardant (K-grades), food grade — specified when the service demands it.

Belt profile

  • Smooth — standard, up to about 18–20° incline.
  • Chevron / patterned — raised V-profile, up to about 30–35°.
  • Cleated with sidewalls — up to 90°; capacity calculated by bucket volume, not by trough cross-section.

Lump size constraint

Belt width must also satisfy the lump size. A practical rule for a 35° trough is B ≥ 3.3amax + 200 mm for unsized material (up to 10 % lumps), or B ≥ 2amax + 200 mm for uniformly sized material, where amax is the largest lump dimension. A belt that is wide enough for the tonnage may still be too narrow for the rock.

1.7 Capacity Calculation

Capacity is the product of the material cross-section on the belt and the belt speed. Everything hinges on computing that cross-section correctly.

Material properties that drive the cross-section

  • Bulk density ρ (kg/m³) — use the loose (as-conveyed) density, not the compacted density.
  • Angle of repose φ — the angle of a freely poured heap at rest.
  • Surcharge angle θ — the angle the material surface takes on a moving belt. Vibration flattens the heap, so θ is typically 5–15° less than φ. Common design values are 5°, 10°, 20° and 25°.
  • Largest lump size, abrasiveness, moisture, temperature and corrosivity — these govern belt and cover selection rather than capacity.

Usable belt width

Material must not reach the belt edge, or it spills. ISO 5048 defines a usable width:

b = 0.9B − 0.05  (m), for B ≤ 2 m     b = B − 0.25  (m), for B > 2 m (1.2)

Cross-sectional area

The loaded cross-section splits into a trapezoid S2 formed by the trough, and a cap S1 above the chord, formed by the surcharge angle.

With w the chord (top) width of the trapezoid:

w = l3 + (b − l3) cos λ (1.3)
S2 = [ l3 + (b − l3) cos λ2 ] · (b − l3) sin λ2 (1.4)
S1 = w2 tan θ6 (1.5)
S = S1 + S2  (m²) (1.6)
Check the limiting cases

Equation 1.4 must collapse correctly. At λ = 0 (a flat belt) sin λ = 0 and S2 = 0, leaving only the surcharge cap on a flat belt — correct. At λ = 90° it gives l3(b − l3)/2, the area of a rectangular channel — also correct. Always run this test on a formula you have transcribed.

1.8 Belt Speed Selection

For a given tonnage, a faster belt can be narrower — and narrower belts are much cheaper. But speed costs elsewhere: more wear, more dust and spillage, more degradation of friable material, higher impact at transfer points, and more difficult tracking. Speed selection is therefore bounded above by the material and the belt width, not by the arithmetic.

Special cases

Reduce speed to 1.0–1.5 m/s for very dusty material, for fragile products (coke, briquettes, packaged goods), where a belt weigher demands a steady stream, or where the belt discharges into a confined chute. Raise the belt width instead.

1.9 Motion Resistances and Power

The drive must overcome four categories of resistance. ISO 5048 and DIN 22101 group them as follows.

SymbolResistancePhysical origin
FHMainIdler bearing friction, indentation rolling resistance of the belt on the rolls, and flexure of belt and material. Distributed over the whole length.
FNSecondaryInertia and friction at the loading point, skirtboard friction, belt wrap resistance on pulleys, pulley bearing friction. Concentrated at the terminals.
FStSlope (lift)Raising the material through the height H. Negative on a downhill conveyor.
FSSpecialBelt cleaners, ploughs, trippers, extended skirtboards, tilted idlers.

Distributed masses

All resistance calculations run on mass per unit length (kg/m):

qG = Q3.6 v  (kg/m, with Q in t/h and v in m/s) (1.9)
  • qB — belt mass per metre, from the belt catalogue.
  • qRO = (mass of one carrying idler set) / ao.
  • qRU = (mass of one return idler set) / au.

Main and secondary resistance

ISO 5048 lumps the secondary resistance into a length coefficient C, giving the compact form:

FH + FN = C f L g [ qRO + qRU + (2qB + qG) cos δ ] (1.10)

Slope resistance

FSt = qG · H · g  (N) (1.11)

Note that only the material mass appears. The belt is an endless loop: what goes up on the carrying run comes down on the return run, so its net lift work is zero.

Peripheral force and power

FU = (FH + FN) + FSt + FS (1.12)
PA = FU · v  (W)     PM = PAη  (η ≈ 0.85–0.95) (1.13)
Sign convention on downhill conveyors

If FSt is negative and larger in magnitude than the friction resistances, then FU < 0: the conveyor is regenerative and the motor acts as a generator. Such a conveyor needs a brake, not a backstop, and the drive must be rated for the braking duty. Never allow a spreadsheet to report a negative power as "no motor required."

1.10 Belt Tension Analysis

The peripheral force FU is transmitted by friction over the wrap of the drive pulley. The classical capstan (Euler–Eytelwein) relation sets the limit:

T1T2 ≤ eμθ      FU = T1 − T2 (1.14)

Combining, the minimum slack-side tension needed to avoid slip is

T2,min = FU · 1eμθ − 1 = FU · Cw      T1 = T2 + FU (1.15)

Two things follow immediately. First, lagging pays twice: raising μ from 0.25 to 0.35 at 210° wrap cuts the required T2 by about 43 %, which cuts T1, which cuts the required belt strength. Second, a dual drive (two lagged pulleys in series) is the standard answer when the belt strength required by a single drive becomes uneconomic.

Starting condition

Equation 1.15 applies to steady running. At start-up the drive must also accelerate the belt and material, and the peak tension rises to roughly 1.3–1.5 FU for a direct-on-line start. Either check the belt against this starting tension or fit controlled starting (fluid coupling, soft starter, VFD) so that it never occurs.

1.11 Belt Sag and Take-up

Between two idler sets the loaded belt hangs as a catenary. Too much sag and the material is repeatedly lifted and dropped at every idler: energy is wasted, material spills, and the belt flexes itself to death. The sag ratio is limited to 1–2 % of the idler pitch, which sets a minimum tension everywhere on the carrying run:

Tmin ≥ (qB + qG) g ao8 (h/a)max  = 6.25 (qB + qG) g ao for 2 % sag (1.16)

On the return run the same expression applies with qG = 0 and au in place of ao.

The governing tension

The take-up must satisfy both the no-slip requirement (Eq. 1.15) and the sag requirement (Eq. 1.16), at every point and in every operating case — running, starting, stopping, empty and fully loaded. The design tension is the maximum over all of these. On short inclined conveyors the sag limit usually governs; on long flights the drive-slip limit usually governs.

Take-up types

TypePrincipleUse
Screw (manual)Threaded rod moves the tail pulleyShort conveyors, up to about 60 m. Cheap, but tension is not maintained automatically as the belt stretches.

Take-up travel

The travel must absorb elastic stretch, permanent elongation and splicing allowance:

  • Fabric (EP) belts: allow roughly 1.5 % of the total belt length, i.e. 1.5 % of 2L. The vertical pulley movement is half of that, because the belt is doubled around the pulley.
  • Steel cord belts: about 0.3 % of 2L.
  • Add 0.5–1.0 m of extra travel for re-splicing.

The belt is selected on tension per unit width at the highest-tension point (normally T1 at the drive):

kreq = Tmax · SFB  (N/mm) (1.17)

  2. Transverse rigidity — the belt must be stiff enough not to collapse between the wing rolls when empty.

1.13 Conveyor Geometry

Maximum inclination

The limit is material sliding back on the belt, not motor power. Practical maxima are given in Table 1.7; as a guide, take the surcharge angle θ as an approximate upper bound for a smooth belt. Lumpy, rounded, wet or dusty material must be de-rated further.

Transition distance

Between the last fully troughed idler and the terminal pulley the belt flattens. During that transition the belt edges must travel further than the centre, so they stretch. If the transition is too short the edge stress can exceed the working tension by several times, and the belt splits along the edge.

  • Fabric belt, 35° trough, pulley at trough depth: transition length ≈ 2B.
  • Fabric belt, 35° trough, pulley at half trough depth: ≈ 1B.
  • Steel cord belt: 3–4B — steel cord will not tolerate differential stretch.

Vertical curves

A concave curve (the belt profile is a valley, e.g. at the bottom of an incline) risks the belt lifting off the idlers when tension is high and the belt is empty. The radius must satisfy:

Rconcave ≥ TmaxqB g (1.18)

A convex curve (a hump) presses the belt into the idlers and overstresses the belt edges. A common rule of thumb for a 35° troughed fabric belt is Rconvex ≥ 12.5B; steel cord belts need considerably more. Both cases must ultimately be checked against the manufacturer's data.

1.14 Belt Tracking and Troubleshooting

A belt runs toward the side it reaches first. That single sentence explains most mistracking: any misalignment, buildup or asymmetric load steers the belt. Correct in this order — structure first, then pulleys, then idlers, then loading, and only last consider training idlers.

1.15 Safety and Maintenance

Required safety provisions

  • Emergency pull-cord (lanyard) switches along the full accessible length, on both sides where access permits, reachable from any point of the walkway.
  • Nip guards at every in-running nip: drive and tail pulleys, snub and bend pulleys, take-up.
  • Backstop on any inclined conveyor that could run back when loaded.
  • Belt drift (misalignment) switches and belt slip / underspeed detection.
  • Start warning — audible and visual, with a delay, before any remote start.
  • Lockout / tagout on the isolator before any work on the conveyor. The stored energy in a gravity take-up must be secured before releasing belt tension.
  • Guarded crossings — never step over or crawl under a running belt.
The two classic fatalities

Conveyor fatalities cluster around two scenarios: someone clearing a blockage or cleaning near a running belt and being drawn into a nip point; and someone releasing belt tension without securing the take-up counterweight, which then falls. Both are eliminated by disciplined isolation and by designing cleaning access that does not require reaching near the belt.

δ = 5.74°    k = 0.99

Step 2 — Trial belt width and cross-section

Check the lump constraint first: for unsized material, B ≥ 3.3(100) + 200 = 530 mm. Try B = 800 mm, which satisfies this with margin.

Usable width, Eq. 1.2: b = 0.9(0.8) − 0.05 = 0.670 m.
Idler roll length, Table 1.4: l3 = 0.315 m.
So b − l3 = 0.355 m.

Chord width, Eq. 1.3:
w = 0.315 + 0.355(cos 35°) = 0.315 + 0.355(0.8192) = 0.6058 m

Trapezoid, Eq. 1.4:
S2 = [0.315 + 0.2908/2] × [0.355(0.5736)/2] = (0.4604)(0.10181) = 0.04687 m²

Surcharge cap, Eq. 1.5:
S1 = (0.6058)²(tan 20°)/6 = (0.36699)(0.36397)/6 = 0.02226 m²

S = 0.04687 + 0.02226 = 0.0691 m²

Step 3 — Belt speed

Rearranging Eq. 1.8 for the required speed:

v = Q / (3.6 S ρ k) = 500 / (3.6 × 0.0691 × 1600 × 0.99) = 1.27 m/s

The next preferred speed is 1.6 m/s. Table 1.9 permits up to 2.5 m/s for an 800 mm belt on medium-abrasive material, so 1.6 m/s is acceptable.

Rated capacity at this speed:
Qrated = 3.6(0.0691)(1.6)(1600)(0.99) = 631 t/h

v = 1.6 m/s    Qrated = 631 t/h    utilisation = 500/631 = 79 %

A 21 % margin is reasonable: it absorbs surge from the crusher and allows for a denser or wetter feed than assumed.

Step 4 — Distributed masses

Material, Eq. 1.9 — use the design tonnage, not the rated capacity:
qG = 500 / (3.6 × 1.6) = 86.8 kg/m

Belt: trial EP 400/3 with 4 + 2 mm covers, 800 mm wide → qB ≈ 13 kg/m

Carrying idlers: 21 kg per set at ao = 1.2 m (Table 1.5) → qRO = 21/1.2 = 17.5 kg/m

Return idlers: 13 kg per set at au = 3.0 m → qRU = 13/3.0 = 4.3 kg/m

Step 5 — Main and secondary resistance

From Table 1.10 at L = 120 m, C = 1.70. Outdoor abrasive duty, well maintained: f = 0.020 (Table 1.11).

Bracket term:
qRO + qRU + (2qB + qG)cos δ
= 17.5 + 4.3 + (26 + 86.8)(0.995) = 21.8 + 112.2 = 134.0 kg/m

Leading term: C f L g = 1.70(0.020)(120)(9.81) = 40.02

FH + FN = 40.02 × 134.0 = 5365 N = 5.37 kN

Step 6 — Slope and special resistance

FSt = qG H g = 86.8(12)(9.81) = 10 219 N = 10.22 kN

Special resistances — two belt cleaners, a V-plough and 3 m of skirtboard — estimated at 6 % of the sum above: FS ≈ 0.06(5.37 + 10.22) = 0.94 kN

FU = 5.37 + 10.22 + 0.94 = 16.53 kN

Note that the lift accounts for 62 % of the total force. On an inclined conveyor the useful work dominates, which is exactly why belt conveyors are efficient.

Step 7 — Power and motor

PA = FU v = 16 530(1.6) = 26 448 W = 26.4 kW at the drive pulley

Drive train efficiency (reducer, couplings, backstop) η = 0.85:
PM = 26.4/0.85 = 31.1 kW

Select a 37 kW, 4-pole motor with a fluid coupling and a backstop

A 30 kW motor would be running at 104 % of rating — unacceptable. The backstop is mandatory: with 12 m of lift the loaded belt would otherwise run back on power failure.

Step 8 — Belt tensions

Drive pulley: rubber lagged, wet outdoor service → μ = 0.35 (Table 1.6). Snub pulley gives a wrap of 210° = 3.665 rad.

eμθ = e0.35(3.665) = e1.283 = 3.61, so Cw = 1/(3.61 − 1) = 0.383 (agrees with Table 1.12).

No-slip requirement:
T2 = 16 530(0.383) = 6.34 kN

Sag requirement on the carrying run, Eq. 1.16 at 2 % sag:
Tmin = 6.25(13 + 86.8)(9.81)(1.2) = 7.34 kN

The sag limit governs. Set the take-up so that no point on the carrying run falls below 7.34 kN.

T2 = 7.34 kN    T1 = 7.34 + 16.53 = 23.9 kN

In detailed design

A full tension walk around the loop — segment by segment, for empty, part-loaded, starting and stopping cases — is required to locate the true minimum and maximum. The two-point check above is the sizing estimate, not the final answer.

Step 9 — Belt selection

Tension per unit width: Tmax/B = 23.9/0.8 = 29.9 kN/m = 29.9 N/mm

Fabric belt with a vulcanised splice, SF = 10 (Table 1.13):
kreq = 29.9 × 10 = 299 N/mm

The next standard fabric belt is EP 315/3. Selecting one size up for cover-wear margin and to match the mass assumed in Step 4:

EP 400/3, 800 mm wide, 4 + 2 mm grade W covers

Utilisation of belt strength = 299/400 = 75 %, so the true safety factor is 13.4 — comfortable for abrasive duty with impact loading.

Step 10 — Take-up and pulleys

Gravity take-up force = 2T2 = 2(7.34) = 14.7 kN
Counterweight mass = 14 700/9.81 = 1500 kg

Take-up travel: belt length 2L = 240 m; at 1.5 % elongation = 3.6 m of belt, so vertical travel = 1.8 m. Adding 0.7 m for re-splicing:

Take-up travel = 2.5 m, counterweight 1500 kg

Pulleys: select 500 mm drive, 400 mm tail, 315 mm snub and bend. Check the drive pulley face pressure, Eq. 1.1:

p = (23 900 + 7340)/(500 × 800) = 31 240/400 000 = 0.078 N/mm² < 0.4 N/mm²  ✓

Step 11 — Backstop

On power failure the net runback force is the lift resistance less the friction that opposes it:
Fhold = 10.22 − 5.37 = 4.85 kN

Holding torque at the drive pulley = 4.85 × 0.25 = 1.21 kN·m. Select a backstop rated at or above this, applied at the reducer high-speed or low-speed shaft as the manufacturer specifies.

1.18 Problems

  1. Capacity. A 1000 mm belt runs on 35° three-roll idlers at 2.0 m/s, carrying bituminous coal (ρ = 800 kg/m³, θ = 20°) on a horizontal flight. Compute b, w, S1, S2 and the mass capacity in t/h. [Answer: S1 = 0.0355 m², S2 = 0.0772 m², S = 0.113 m², Q ≈ 649 t/h]
  2. Effect of trough angle. Repeat Problem 1 for trough angles of 20°, 30° and 45°. Plot S against λ and comment on why 35° is the most common choice in practice even though 45° gives more area.
  3. Width selection. A plant must move 1200 t/h of iron ore (ρ = 2600 kg/m³, θ = 20°, max lump 200 mm) on a 35° trough. Select a belt width and a preferred speed, and justify both against Table 1.9 and the lump size rule.
  4. Power. A horizontal conveyor is 250 m long and moves 800 t/h at 2.5 m/s. Take qB = 22 kg/m, qRO = 20 kg/m, qRU = 7 kg/m, f = 0.020. Compute FU, PA and the motor power at η = 0.88.
  5. Effect of lift. Repeat Problem 4 with a 25 m lift over the same 250 m length. By what percentage does the required power increase, and what fraction of the total force is now useful lift work? Comment on the implications for a downhill version of the same conveyor.
  6. Drive and tensions. For the conveyor of Problem 5, size the drive assuming a rubber-lagged pulley (μ = 0.35) with 210° wrap. Compute T2 and T1, check the sag limit at ao = 1.2 m and 2 % sag, and select a fabric belt for a 1200 mm width at SF = 10.
  7. Lagging economics. For Problem 6, recompute T1 if the pulley were bare steel in wet conditions (μ = 0.10). What belt strength would then be needed, and what does this tell you about the value of lagging?
  8. Take-up. A 400 m conveyor uses a nylon (NN) belt with 2.5 % total elongation. Determine the required gravity take-up travel, and compare with the same conveyor using a steel cord belt at 0.3 %. Discuss the capital cost trade-off.
  9. Failure analysis. A conveyor mistracks consistently toward the drive side, but only over the last 30 m before the head pulley, and only when loaded. List the three most probable causes in order and state the diagnostic test you would run for each.
  10. Design synthesis. A quarry must lift 350 t/h of crushed granite (ρ = 1500 kg/m³, max lump 150 mm) through 22 m of height over a horizontal distance of 95 m. Carry out a complete design following Section 1.16 and state every assumption you make. Check the inclination against Table 1.7 before you begin — if it fails, propose and size an alternative arrangement.
