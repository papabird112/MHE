1.1 What Handling Is, and Whether It Adds Value

Materials handling is “short-distance movement that usually takes place within the confines of a building such as a plant or a warehouse, and between a building and a transportation agency.” The distance qualifier is what separates handling from transportation: moving a pallet across a warehouse is handling, shipping the same pallet across the country is transport.

Economists describe what a process adds in terms of utility. Manufacturing creates form utility — it changes the shape, form and makeup of the material. Handling, storage and control create time and place utility — they put the material where it is wanted, when it is wanted, without changing what it is.

A refinement of Chapter 3

Chapter 3 states flatly that handling adds no value. That is the traditional teaching, and as a design attitude it is useful — it stops you gold-plating a conveyor. But it is not strictly true, and it is worth knowing why. Time and place utility can add real value:

  • The value to a customer of overnight delivery is at least equal to what they pay for it, otherwise they would have used ordinary post.
  • The value of storing parts beside a bottleneck machine is the gain from higher machine utilisation, less the cost of storing them there.

So handling can add value. The reason we still design it as a cost is practical, not philosophical: the benefits are hard to identify and quantify, while the costs — equipment, indirect labour, floor space — are easy. That asymmetry, not a claim about value, is what drives the cost-minimising approach in §1.3.

The ten principles

The ten principles of the College-Industry Council on Material Handling Education are set out with the belt conveyor in Chapter 3 — see §3.1, Table 3.1 — and they apply to everything in this chapter, so they are not repeated here. Three of them do most of the work in this chapter. The Unit Load principle (§1.6) governs how material is presented to the equipment. The Standardisation principle argues against the very variety catalogued here — every extra equipment type is another set of spares and another training course. And the Life Cycle Cost principle is what turns a technical shortlist into a single choice in §1.19.

1.2 The Materials Handling Equation

Before any equipment can be chosen, six questions have to be answered in order. Together they are known as the materials handling equation.

Table 1.1. The six questions, and what each one settles.
#QuestionWhat it establishes
1Why is the material moved? Whether the move is needed at all. The Work principle says the cheapest move is the one you delete.
2What is moved? The material: state, size, weight, shape, lump size, flowability, abrasiveness (§1.4, §1.5).
3Where from and where to? The route: distance, level changes, curves, branches, fixed or variable path (§1.8).
4When, and how often? The rate: tonnes or units per hour, continuous or intermittent, peak versus average.
5How is it moved? The method and the equipment — an answer, not a question you start from.
6Who moves it? Operator, semi-automated or fully automated, and the labour cost that follows.

1.3 Which Cost Should Be Minimised

The usual approach is to treat handling as a cost to be minimised: the production process is designed first, alternative handling systems that satisfy it are then generated, and the cheapest is selected. Whether that is the right criterion depends on how much of the rest of the plant is still open to change.

SituationCorrect criterionWhy
A completely new facility and process is being designed Total cost of production The cheapest handling system may force a layout that raises production cost by more than it saves. Handling and layout have to be traded against each other.
The layout and process are fixed and too costly to change Handling system cost alone Everything else is a constraint, so handling cost is the only variable left.

In practice even a greenfield design cannot hold every cost component open at once. The aspects with the largest impact get fixed early and become constraints on everything decided afterwards. Recognising which decisions have already been frozen is half of knowing what you are actually allowed to optimise.

1.4 Material Characteristics and Categories

The characteristics that affect handling are:

  • Size — width, depth, height.
  • Weight — per item, or per unit volume.
  • Shape — round, square, long, rectangular, irregular.
  • Other — slippery, fragile, sticky, explosive, frozen, hot, corrosive, hygroscopic.
Table 1.2. Material categories by physical state.
Material categorySolidLiquidGas
Individual unitsPart, subassembly——
Containerised itemsCarton, bag, tote, box, pallet, binBarrelCylinder
Bulk materialsSand, cement, coal, granular productsLiquid chemicals, solvents, gasolineOxygen, nitrogen, carbon dioxide

The category decides the entire design method, through a chain of three implications:

Individual units and containerised items → discrete material flow → unit loads → unit handling equipment (1.1)
Bulk materials → continuous material flow → bulk handling equipment (1.2)

Discrete flow is designed from a count and a cycle time; continuous flow is designed from a cross-section and a velocity, exactly as Chapter 3 does for belt conveyors. Note that the same physical substance can be handled either way, and the choice is a real design decision rather than a property of the material.

1.6 The Unit Load Concept

A unit load is either a single unit of an item, or multiple units so arranged or restrained that they can be handled as a single unit and maintain their integrity. It is the single most important idea in discrete handling, because it is what converts an unmanageable stream of individual cartons into a countable object that standard equipment can pick up.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
More items handled at once, so fewer trips, and potentially lower handling cost, shorter loading and unloading times, and less product damage Time spent forming and breaking down the load
Enables the use of standardised handling equipment Cost of the pallets, containers and restraining materials
Empty pallets and containers may have to be returned to their point of origin

Restraining a unit load

  • Self-restraining — one or more units that hold together on their own, such as a single part or interlocking parts. No equipment at all.
  • Platforms — pallets (paper, wood, plastic, metal), skids (metal, plastic).
  • Sheets — slipsheets of plastic, cardboard or plywood.
  • Reusable containers — tote pans, pallet boxes, skid boxes, bins, baskets, bulk containers such as barrels, intermodal containers.
  • Disposable containers — cartons, bags, crates.
  • Racks.
  • Load stabilisation — strapping, shrink-wrap, stretch-wrap, glue, tape, wire, rubber bands.

Moving a unit load

There are only four mechanical ways to pick one up, and each one dictates a different family of equipment:

  1. A lifting device under the mass of the load — a pallet and fork truck.
  2. Inserting a lifting element into the body of the load — a ram through a coil of steel.
  3. Squeezing the load between two lifting surfaces — carton clamps on a lift truck, or your own two hands.
  4. Suspending the load — a hoist and crane.

Sizing the load

Unit load design means settling three things: the type, size, weight and configuration of the load; the equipment and method that will handle it; and the methods of forming and breaking it down. For in-process handling the size is bounded above by the batch structure of the plant:

Single part ≤ Unit load size ≤ Transfer batch size ≤ Production batch size (1.4)

A unit load larger than the production batch would have to wait for the next batch of that part to be scheduled — possibly weeks — before it could travel. Within that bound, short moves favour smaller unit loads and long moves favour larger ones. When two operations are adjacent, the unit load may legitimately be a single part.

For distribution the size is bounded by things entirely outside the plant: standard pallet and container sizes, the internal dimensions of truck trailers, rail boxcars and aircraft cargo bays, the existing rack configuration in the warehouse, and the carton sizes and shelf restrictions of the customer. Very little of this is negotiable, which is why distribution unit loads cluster on a handful of standard footprints.

1.7 The Five Equipment Categories

Why the catalogue matters

“If the only tool you have is a hammer, it is amazing how quickly all your problems seem to look like nails.” An engineer who knows three equipment types will design every system out of those three. The purpose of the next twelve sections is breadth, not depth: you are not expected to size any of these machines yet, only to know that they exist and roughly what each is for.

Table 1.7. The five categories. Note that every category has a “no equipment” entry — manual handling is always one of the alternatives on the shortlist.
 CategoryDefinitionSub-categories
ITransport equipment Moves material from one location to another Conveyors, cranes, industrial trucks, and manual
IIPositioning equipment Handles material at a single location, to feed, orient, load, unload or manipulate it into the correct position for what comes next 11 types, §1.11
IIIUnit load formation equipment Restrains material so that it keeps its integrity when handled as a single load 15 types, §1.9
IVStorage equipment Holds or buffers material over a period of time 15 types, §1.17
VIdentification and control equipment Collects and communicates the information that coordinates material flow 7 types, §1.18

The boundaries are not always sharp, and the overlaps are worth noticing. An AS/RS is storage equipment whose S/R machine also transports. A storage carousel does the same. A jib crane is transport equipment that frequently does a positioning job. The category tells you which chapter of the catalogue to open, not what the machine is forbidden to do.

1.8 Conveyor, Crane or Truck?

Within transport equipment, the three sub-categories are distinguished by four functional characteristics of the move, not of the machine:

  • Path — fixed (between two specific points) or variable (between a large variety of points).
  • Area — restricted to a limited area, or unrestricted.
  • Move frequency — high number of moves per period, or low and intermittent.
  • Adjacent move — is the move between adjacent activities or not?

The recurring trade-off

Flexibility and cost per move run in opposite directions. A conveyor has a large fixed cost and a near-zero cost per move; a fork truck has a small fixed cost and a significant cost per move, most of it the operator's wages. That is why the deciding question is almost always flow volume: below some break-even the truck wins, above it the conveyor does. Conveyors are used when there is sufficient flow volume to justify the fixed investment — and that sentence, not any property of the material, is the real selection rule.

Slipsheets versus pallets

A slipsheet costs 10–30 % of a pallet and weighs 1–5 % as much, which makes it very attractive for long-distance shipping where the pallet would otherwise be paid for as freight and then thrown away. The price is slower handling, greater load damage inside the facility, and a push/pull attachment that reduces the lift truck's own load capacity.

Shrink-wrap versus stretch-wrap

In shrink-wrapping a film or bag is placed over the load and heat is applied to shrink it. In stretch-wrapping the film is wound around the load while being stretched. Stretch-wrapping has lower material, labour and energy costs, and has replaced shrink-wrap in most applications.

Palletisers

A stripper plate palletiser assembles a whole layer of cases on an apron, positions the apron over the pallet, then withdraws it so the layer drops as one. That is why its throughput is several times that of a robot placing one or two cases at a time — and also why it cannot cope with a load whose pattern changes every time.

The pallet loading problem

Two distinct optimisation problems share the name:

  • The Manufacturer's Pallet Loading Problem — load identical items onto a pallet so that the number of items per pallet is maximised. The same answer is reused indefinitely, so it is worth considerable effort to solve well, and it can be executed by a stripper plate palletiser.
  • The Distributor's Pallet Loading Problem — load items of various sizes so that the volume loaded is maximised, or so that the number of pallets needed is minimised. Every pallet needs a fresh solution, so the effort spent per solution must be small, and execution is manual or robotic.

Both are NP-hard even under the usual simplifying assumptions that all items and pallets are rectangular and that items are placed orthogonally. That is not a reason to avoid them; it is the reason good heuristics and pattern libraries exist.

1.10 Pallets

A pallet is a platform with enough clearance beneath its top surface for forks to be inserted. It is the most standardised object in the whole of materials handling, and the dimensions of a great deal of other equipment are derived from it.

Dimensions

  • Size is specified as Depth (stringer length) × Width (deckboard length). Height, typically 125 mm, is not quoted.
  • The 48 × 40 in. (1219 × 1016 mm) pallet is by far the most popular in the United States, at 27 % of all pallets with no other size above 5 %. The reason is dimensional: two fit abreast across the 48 in. dimension in a railroad freight car, and two fit abreast across the 40 in. dimension in most trucks.
  • The 1200 × 800 mm Euro-Pallet is the European standard.
  • The maximum depth of a standard pallet is 48 in. That single fact is why the rated load capacity of counterbalanced lift trucks is defined at a 24 in. load centre — half of 48 — as §1.15 will show.

Design features

Table 1.12. The four design choices. The default option is shown in bold.
ChoiceOptionsTrade-off
EntryTwo-way vs four-way Four-way lets forks and strapping enter any side, increasing flexibility and cost. Four-way is achieved by notching the stringers (cheaper, but pallet jacks still get only two-way entry because their wheels cannot pass the notches) or by block legs (four-way for all fork trucks, and nestable for empty storage)
FacesSingle face vs double face Single face is cheaper and nests for empty storage; double face adds strength and stability and spreads the load when pallets are stacked. Most disposable wooden pallets are single face, most reusable ones double face
ReversibilityReversible vs nonreversible A reversible pallet can be loaded either way up, useful where deckboards get soiled — but pallet jacks and pallet trucks cannot be used, because their front wheels have nowhere to extend to the floor
StringerFlush vs single wing vs double wing Wings overhang the stringers and are usually the first point of failure. A single wing gives clearance for the outriggers of a narrow-aisle straddle truck; a double wing (“stevedore's pallet”) leaves room for bar slings so a crane can lift it

The most popular type overall is the two-way, double-face, nonreversible, flush stringer pallet. Not every combination is feasible — a single-face reversible pallet is a contradiction — and several feasible ones are structurally weak or offer no benefit.

Special-purpose pallets

  • Take-it-or-leave-it pallet — fitted with cleats on the top deckboards so forks can pass beneath the load and remove it from the pallet.
  • Slave pallet — a support base for palletised loads inside a rack or conveyor system.
  • Captive pallet — stays within one facility and is never exchanged.
  • Exchange pallet — ownership transfers with the load among a pool of shippers.
  • Shipping pallet — a single one-way trip, then disposed of.

Pallet patterns

The four basic patterns are block (all cases the same way, columns aligned), row, brick and pinwheel. Block stacking gives the highest compressive strength because corner posts line up vertically; interlocked patterns such as brick and pinwheel give a more stable load that is less likely to shed in transit but lose some of that column strength. The choice is a genuine trade-off between stacking height and load stability.

1.11 Positioning Equipment

Positioning equipment handles material at a single location. Compared with manual handling it can raise productivity where handling frequency is high, improve quality and limit damage where the item is heavy or awkward, and reduce fatigue and injury where the environment is hazardous or inaccessible. In many cases it is justified by ergonomics alone.

Table 1.13. The eleven types of positioning equipment.
#TypeFunction and key limit
1Manual Under ideal conditions the maximum recommended weight for manual lifting is 51 lb (23 kg), from the NIOSH 1991 lifting equation, which then applies six multipliers that reduce it for less than ideal tasks
2Lift / tilt / turn table Lifts, tilts or turns a load to limit a worker's reaching and bending. A pallet load leveller lowers as each layer is added so palletising always happens at about 750 mm
3Dock leveller Bridges the height difference between a truck bed and the dock
4Ball transfer table Manual transfer between conveyors and machines in any direction. Ball friction limits the load to about 600 lb (270 kg) because the load is pushed
5Rotary index table Synchronous transfer of small parts between stations around a circular table. Because every part indexes at the same instant, it is difficult to put buffers between stations — one slow station stops all of them
6Parts feeder Feeds and orients small identical parts for automatic assembly. Motion from vibration, gravity, centrifugal force, tumbling or air pressure. The vibratory bowl feeder is the most versatile: parts climb a spiral track and wrongly oriented ones fall back for another attempt

1.12 Conveyors

Conveyors are used when material moves frequently between specific points, over a fixed path, and the flow volume justifies the fixed investment. They are classified three ways at once, and it is worth recording all three for every type:

  • Product — unit load or bulk load
  • Location — in-floor, on-floor or overhead
  • Accumulation — can loads accumulate on the conveyor, or not?
Why accumulation matters

Accumulation is the ability of loads to stop and queue on the conveyor while it keeps running. Without it, every stoppage downstream must immediately stop the whole conveyor and everything feeding it. With it, the conveyor itself becomes a buffer. This single property often decides the choice between two otherwise similar conveyors.

19SortationUnit · On-floor/Overhead Merges, identifies, inducts and separates products to specific destinations. See below

Sortation systems

Throughput is expressed in cartons per minute (CPM), and a sortation system always has three subsystems in series:

  1. Merge — items arrive from picking or receiving on several conveyors and are consolidated for presentation at the induct area.
  2. Induct — each item's destination is identified, by eye or by scanner, and a correct gap is created between items using short variable-speed conveyors before release to the sorter. Gapping is what actually limits CPM; the sorter itself is rarely the bottleneck.
  3. Sort — items are diverted to outbound conveyors for shipping, palletising, staging or a secondary sort.
Table 1.15. The five sortation mechanisms.
MechanismActionNotes
DivertersStationary or movable arms deflect, push or pull the product Never touch the conveyor, so they work with almost any flat-surface conveyor. Simple and low cost
Pop-up devicesRows of powered rollers, wheels or chains rise above the conveyor surface to lift and steer the product Only for flat-bottomed items. Pop-up rollers are generally faster than pop-up wheels
Sliding shoeDiverter slats slide sideways across the surface to guide the product off Also called a moving slat sorter. Gentle and gradual; diverts to either side
Tilting deviceTrays or slats both carry and sort Can accommodate elevation changes. Tilt tray usually in continuous loops with recirculation; tilt slat handles a wider variety of products

The trend that removes the sorter

Sortation exists because a producer ships single-item pallets to a distribution centre, which then has to break them down and rebuild multi-item customer loads. If instead the producer builds a mixed-item pallet per customer at the point of manufacture, the sortation step disappears entirely and cross-docking becomes possible. The cheapest sorter, as with the cheapest move, is the one you eliminate.

1.13 Cranes

Cranes move loads over variable horizontal and vertical paths within a restricted area. They are used where flow volume is insufficient or too intermittent to justify a conveyor. They are more flexible than conveyors and less flexible than industrial trucks, and the loads they handle vary far more in shape and weight than anything a conveyor carries. Most use a hoist for the vertical movement, though a manipulator is substituted where precise positioning is needed.

1.14 Industrial Trucks

An industrial truck is one that is not licensed to travel on public roads; a vehicle that is so licensed is a commercial truck. Industrial trucks move material over variable horizontal paths with no restriction on the area covered, add vertical movement if they have lifting capability, and are used where flow volume will not justify a conveyor. They are the most flexible of the three transport categories.

A lift truck has up to five degrees of freedom: horizontal translation (drive wheels), horizontal rotation (steering), vertical lift (forks along the mast), mast tilt (forward to load, back to travel) and fork translation (to suit different load widths).

1.15 Lift Truck Load Capacity

This is the one calculation in this chapter, and it matters because getting it wrong tips the truck over. The principle is a simple moment balance: the weight of the vehicle and operator behind the front wheels counterbalances the weight of the load and the part of the vehicle beyond the front wheels. The front wheels are the fulcrum.

W = GBF + L = MF + L = W0(F + L0)F + L (1.5)
SymbolQuantity
GEmpty truck weight
BFront axle to truck load centre distance
FFront axle to fork face distance
LActual load centre, from fork face to the load's centre of gravity
L0Rated load centre = 24 in. (500 mm)
MLoad moment, GB
W0Rated load capacity, quoted at L0
ULDUnit load depth
L = ULD2  for a constant-density rectangular load (1.6)
Why 24 inches

The maximum depth of a standard pallet is 48 in. (§1.10). A constant-density load on it has its centre of gravity at 24 in. from the fork face — so the rated load centre is simply half of the deepest standard pallet. Rated capacities run from 1,000 to 100,000 lb, quoted at L0 = 24 in. and lift heights up to 13 ft. A truck could equally be specified by its load moment M, which would be more fundamental, but a weight in kilograms is easier for an operator to relate to than a moment in newton-metres.

Derating and rerating

Two different adjustments arise:

  • Derating (W < W0) — the permissible load weight when the load's centre of gravity exceeds the 24 in. rated centre. The truck has not changed; the load has.
  • Rerating (a new W0 at L = L0) — a change to the truck itself: extra counterweight, a fork attachment, an extended mast.
W0′ = M ± ΔMF′ + L0 (1.7)

A further correction applies to lift height: the rated load moment should be reduced by 4,800 in·lb for every foot of lift above 13 ft. Masts reach up to 40 ft in single, double, triple and quad-stage forms; the free lift is the fork movement before the mast itself begins to extend, which is what matters when driving through a doorway or inside a trailer.

Example 2.1 — Derating for a deep load

A truck rated W0 = 3,000 lb has F = 12 in. What can it carry if the unit load is 60 in. deep and of constant density?

Step 1 — Find the actual load centre

L = ULD / 2 = 60 / 2 = 30 in., which exceeds the 24 in. rated centre, so the truck must be derated.

Step 2 — Apply Eq. 1.5

W = 3,000(12 + 24) / (12 + 30) = 108,000 / 42

W = 2,571 lb

The truck has lost 14 % of its rated capacity purely because the load sits further forward. The load moment 108,000 in·lb is unchanged — only its distribution has moved.

Example 2.2 — Rerating by adding counterweight

The same truck (F = 12 in., W0 = 3,000 lb) has a 500 lb counterweight bolted to the rear at 72 in. from the front axle. What is the new rated capacity?

Step 1 — Original and added moment

M = W0(F + L0) = 3,000(12 + 24) = 108,000 in·lb
ΔM = 500 × 72 = 36,000 in·lb

Step 2 — Apply Eq. 1.7

W0′ = (108,000 + 36,000) / (12 + 24) = 144,000 / 36

W0′ = 4,000 lb

500 lb of counterweight bought 1,000 lb of capacity, because it acts at twice the lever arm of the load. That leverage is the whole idea of a counterbalanced truck — but note that the tyres, axle and brakes must also be rated for the extra 1,500 lb now standing on them.

Example 2.3 — Rerating for a fork attachment

A truck is rated 2,500 lb with F = 12 in. Its forks are replaced by carton clamps. The clamps weigh 225 lb with their centre of gravity 30 in. from the front axle; the forks they replace weighed 100 lb at 42 in. The distance from the front axle to the clamp face is 15 in. Find the new rated capacity.

Step 1 — Original moment

M = 2,500(12 + 24) = 90,000 in·lb

Step 2 — Net moment change from the swap

The clamps add moment; the removed forks take moment away.
ΔM = (225 × 30) − (100 × 42) = 6,750 − 4,200 = 2,550 in·lb of lost counterbalance

Step 3 — Apply Eq. 1.7 with the new fork face distance

W0′ = (90,000 − 2,550) / (15 + 24) = 87,450 / 39

W0′ = 2,242 lb

The truck has lost 10 % of its capacity, from two causes at once: the attachment is heavier than what it replaced and sits further forward, and it pushes the load face 3 in. further out. Both effects work the same way. This is why a lift truck must be re-plated whenever an attachment is fitted, and why an operator working from the original data plate would be overloading it.

AS/RS in more detail

An AS/RS integrates the storage medium, a transport mechanism and controls. The S/R machine works in a narrow aisle serving racks on both sides, and travels horizontally and vertically simultaneously — which is why its cycle time is governed by the larger of the two travel times, not their sum. It is usually captive to one aisle; where throughput does not justify one machine per aisle, a transfer car moves it between aisles. Racks run 40–80 ft or higher and demand tight tolerances and level floors, which is what makes them costly; they may be freestanding or rack-supported, carrying the building itself.

Stage 1: technical feasibility

Select the equipment types that can satisfy the material flow requirements at all, from a purely technological point of view. Stage 2: economic feasibility

From among the technically feasible types, choose the one that is most cost effective for the given requirements. The classic illustration: a pallet jack and a pallet truck are both technically feasible for a long-distance move, but the pallet truck — although it costs more to buy — is more cost effective, because the operator rides and so travels faster, and operator time is the dominant cost. The cheaper machine loses on total cost.

Where students most often go wrong

Two errors recur. The first is skipping stage 1 and comparing costs of options that cannot do the job. The second is running stage 2 on purchase price rather than life-cycle cost — which is principle 10 of §3.1, and which for non-automated equipment is dominated not by the machine at all but by the operator's wages over ten years.

1.20 Worked Selection Example

Problem statement

A rice mill receives paddy in 50 kg bags and must move it from an intake shed to a cleaning plant 45 m away, with a 6 m rise into the cleaner's feed hopper. Management is considering whether to keep handling the paddy in bags or to switch to bulk. Required throughput is 25 t/h during a harvest campaign of 90 days a year, two shifts a day. Paddy is granular, 5–10 mm, bulk density 560 kg/m³, angle of repose about 36°, non-abrasive but dusty.

Note that where and when together already point strongly at a conveyor: a fixed path, a restricted area and a high move frequency is the top-left cell of Table 1.8.

Step 2 — Classify the material (§1.4, §1.5)

Paddy is a bulk material in the solid state, so by Eq. 1.2 it implies continuous flow and bulk handling equipment — if we choose to handle it as bulk. In bags it becomes a containerised item and Eq. 1.1 applies instead. Both routes are open, exactly as in Figure 1.2.

Step 4 — Check the unit load alternative honestly (§1.6)

At 25 t/h in 50 kg bags the plant must form and break 500 bags per hour. On a 48 × 40 in. pallet at, say, 40 bags per pallet, that is 12.5 pallets per hour, so roughly one fork truck round trip every five minutes plus two manual palletising stations. The bags themselves, consumed at 500/h for 1,440 h/year, are 720,000 bags a year.

That is the real cost of the unit load route, and it is exactly the disadvantage listed in §1.6: the time spent forming and breaking down the load, plus the cost of the load-restraining material. Bagging is justified when the paddy must be stored by lot, traded, or moved by a variable path — none of which applies to a fixed 45 m link into a cleaner.

Reject the bagged route on economic grounds, not technical ones

A selection is only as good as its stated assumptions. Here they are: 25 t/h is a firm peak, not an average; the route stays straight; the campaign is genuinely only 90 days, so annual energy cost is modest; and no requirement exists to store paddy by lot between intake and cleaning. If any of these changes — particularly the last, which would reintroduce the case for bags — the selection must be revisited.

1.22 Problems

Check yourself first

Before working these, try the 40-question self-check. It marks each answer immediately and links back to the section the question came from.

  1. The handling equation. A bakery moves trays of dough from a mixing room to twelve proving cabinets scattered around a 40 × 25 m production hall, roughly 90 times a shift, with the destination varying each time. Answer all six questions of §1.2, then use Table 1.8 to identify the equipment category. Justify why a conveyor is the wrong answer here despite the high move frequency.
  2. Material code. Build the code for each material and state one design consequence of each character: (a) Portland cement, dusty, repose 39°, abrasive, 1500 kg/m³; (b) wood chips, 40 mm, repose 45°, non-abrasive, 300 kg/m³, light and fluffy; (c) crushed limestone from §3.16, 100 mm lumps, repose 38°, abrasive, 1600 kg/m³.
  3. Reading a code. A supplier quotes a material as D 3 8 J MSX. Without being told what the material is, list every conclusion you can draw about the conveyor that will handle it, and name one candidate material that would fit the code.
  4. Unit load or bulk? For each case decide whether the material should be handled as a unit load or as bulk, and give the deciding reason: (a) cement from a silo to a mixer 30 m away; (b) cement sold in 40 kg bags to builders' merchants; (c) fertiliser from a ship to a store 800 m away; (d) 20 kg boxes of frozen prawns moving between two adjacent freezer rooms.
  5. Unit load sizing. A machine shop runs production batches of 600 parts. Transfer batches are 150 parts and a tote pan holds 25. Check the inequality of Eq. 1.4 and state what would go wrong if management standardised on a tote holding 800 parts.
  6. Pallet choice. Explain which pallet design features are required for each situation, using Table 1.12: (a) loads are handled by a narrow-aisle straddle truck with the pallet on the floor; (b) drums occasionally leak onto the deck; (c) loads must be lifted by crane using bar slings; (d) pallets are used once and shipped abroad.
  7. Pallet dimensions. Explain why a 48 × 40 in. pallet is not the same object as a 40 × 48 in. pallet, and why 48 × 40 became the dominant size in the United States. Then explain the connection between the 48 in. maximum standard pallet depth and the 24 in. rated load centre of a lift truck.
  8. Derating. A truck rated 4,000 lb has F = 14 in. What can it lift if the unit load is 72 in. deep and of constant density? [Answer: 3,040 lb]
  9. Rerating, added counterweight. A truck rated 5,000 lb with F = 12 in. has an 800 lb counterweight added at 66 in. from the front axle. Find the new rated capacity, and state two components that must be checked before the truck is used at that rating. [Answer: 6,467 lb]
  10. Rerating, attachment. A truck rated 3,500 lb with F = 12 in. has its 120 lb forks (centre of gravity 44 in. from the front axle) replaced by a 340 lb roll clamp (centre of gravity 28 in. from the front axle). The clamp face is 17 in. from the front axle. Find the new rated capacity and express the loss as a percentage.
  11. Combined. Using the truck from Problem 10 after rerating, what is the heaviest paper roll 1.2 m in diameter it may carry, assuming the clamp grips it centrally? State every assumption.
  12. Lift height. A truck is rated 3,500 lb at a 24 in. load centre with F = 12 in., so its rated load moment is 126,000 in·lb, valid to a 13 ft lift. What is its allowable capacity at a 24 in. load centre when lifting to 20 ft? [Hint: reduce the rated moment by 4,800 in·lb per foot above 13 ft. Answer: 2,567 lb]
  13. Conveyor classification. For each of these duties, name a conveyor type and give its Unit/Bulk, location and accumulation classification: (a) cartons must queue before a labelling machine; (b) hot castings must move 8 m from a shakeout; (c) car bodies must hang above the floor through a paint shop; (d) cash must travel between a bank counter and a drive-in stall.
  14. Accumulation. Explain why a flat belt conveyor cannot accumulate and a live roller conveyor can, and describe one situation in which the inability to accumulate would shut down a production line.
  15. Sortation. A distribution centre must sort 90 cartons per minute to 24 destinations. Which sortation mechanisms from Table 1.15 are candidates, and which subsystem would you expect to limit throughput? Then explain the circumstances under which the whole sorter could be eliminated.
  16. Narrow aisle economics. A warehouse has 12 aisles, each 60 m long. Changing from a sit-down counterbalanced truck to a narrow-aisle reach truck reduces aisle width from 3.9 m to 2.7 m. How much floor area is released, and what could it hold? What must be checked before assuming the change is worth making?
  17. Storage selection. Use Table 1.24 to choose a storage type for each: (a) 40 pallets per SKU, no shelf life, throughput must be high; (b) 2 pallets per SKU, 900 SKUs, every load must be accessible; (c) 8 pallets per SKU of a product with a 30-day shelf life; (d) archived documents in a space-constrained basement.
  18. Honeycomb loss. Explain what honeycomb loss is, why block stacking and drive-in racks suffer from it while push-back racks do not, and estimate the loss for an item held at 12 pallets in lanes 6 deep.
  19. AS/RS cycle time. An S/R machine travels horizontally at 3 m/s and vertically at 1 m/s. A slot is 60 m along the aisle and 18 m up. How long does the machine take to reach it, and why is the answer not the sum of the two travel times?
  20. Two-stage selection. A vaccine manufacturer must move sealed trays of vials 25 m between two cleanrooms of different classifications, 40 times an hour. Work through both stages of §1.19, stating the technical filters first and the economic comparison second, and identify which identification and control equipment from §1.18 the choice implies.
