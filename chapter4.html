A bucket elevator lifts bulk material straight up, in a footprint barely larger than the casing itself. Nothing else moves a large tonnage vertically for so little power. This chapter builds the design chain — choose the discharge type, size the buckets and spacing, pick the speed that actually discharges, find the power and the tensions, and then deal with the dust, because the elevator leg is the classic location of a grain dust explosion.

4.1 What a Bucket Elevator Is

A bucket elevator is a series of buckets bolted to an endless belt or chain, running in a vertical casing between a driven head pulley (or sprocket) at the top and a take-up pulley in the boot at the bottom. The buckets pick the material up at the boot, carry it up the inside of one casing leg, and throw or pour it out at the head. The empty buckets return down the other leg.

Two facts about that arrangement explain most of the machine's character. First, the material is carried in discrete containers rather than in a continuous stream, so the elevator is indifferent to lift height — a 40 m machine works exactly like a 10 m one, with more buckets in the casing. Second, the only energy the machine must spend is the energy of lifting, and lifting one tonne one metre takes 2.725 W per t/h. That is why bucket elevators are still the cheapest way to raise grain, cement, fertiliser or ash, and why the alternatives get chosen for reasons other than energy.

Table 4.1. Ways of moving bulk material vertically, and why a bucket elevator usually wins.
MachinePractical liftEnergy per tonne per metre liftedWhy it is chosen instead

Vertical screw conveyor (§2.19)To about 10 m3–6 times a bucket elevatorTotally enclosed, no dust, compact, self-feeding from a bin

Steep-angle belt or sandwich belt (§3.10)Any, at up to 30–90°Close to a bucket elevatorLarge lumps; continuous flow; no transfer at the bottom

Note the first row of that table against the third. A pneumatic system can cost ten to thirty times as much energy for the same lift, and it is still chosen every day — because routing, enclosure and the number of delivery points are worth more to the plant than the electricity bill. That is the two-stage selection logic of §1.19 doing its work: technical feasibility first, then total cost, and energy is only part of cost.

The last limitation on the right deserves its own sentence, because it is what kills people. A bucket elevator handling a combustible dust is a vertical, confined, dust-laden space containing a machine that can generate sparks and hot bearings. §4.18 treats it as a design problem with standards attached, not as an operating precaution.

4.3 Anatomy of a Bucket Elevator

Read the figure as a load path and a material path at the same time. The material path is inlet → boot → bucket → up leg → head → chute. The load path is head shaft → head pulley or sprocket → belt or chain → bucket bolts → bucket. Every mechanical failure in service is somewhere on the second path, and the bucket bolts are its weakest link — which is why they are checked, torqued and logged rather than assumed.

The part you cannot see

The boot is where the machine is loaded, where spilt material collects, and where a plug starts. It is also the part nobody opens because it is at floor level behind a guard. A boot that is never cleaned out fills with fines, the buckets start to drag through a compacted bed, the digging resistance climbs, and the first sign is a tripped overload or a slipping belt. Design the boot with a cleanout door, and put it on the maintenance schedule of §4.19.

4.4 The Four Discharge Types

Everything about the machine follows from how the material is intended to leave the bucket at the head. There are four answers, and they are not interchangeable.

Table 4.4. The four discharge types and what each is for.
TypeSpeedBucket and spacingUse it forIt fails when
Centrifugal1.2–3.5 m/sSpaced buckets, pitch 2–3 × projection Free-flowing, non-fragile, mildly abrasive material: grain, sand, cement, fertiliser. The default The material is fragile, sticky, or so light that it blows around inside the casing
Continuous0.4–1.25 m/sBuckets nose to tail, no gap Fragile material, hot material, large lumps, and any duty where degradation or dust must be minimised Capacity per unit speed is needed; a continuous elevator is a larger machine for the same tonnage
Positive0.3–0.6 m/sSpaced buckets on two chains, snubbed under the head Sticky material that will not leave a bucket on its own: wet ash, filter cake, sludge Anything else; it is slow, tall and expensive, and only justified by discharge certainty
Internal / super-capacity0.5–1.0 m/sVery deep buckets between two chains, projecting inward Very high tonnage at modest speed; mine and port duty, 500–2,000 t/h Headroom, cost or lump size do not justify it

The trade is visible in the speed column. Centrifugal discharge buys capacity with speed, and pays for it in degradation, wear and dust. Continuous discharge buys gentleness with a bigger, slower machine. Positive discharge buys certainty with a snub pulley and a great deal of height. Choosing the type is step 2 of Table 4.3 precisely because every later number depends on it.

4.5 The Pole Point: Predicting the Discharge

A designer who chooses “centrifugal discharge” has not yet made the machine discharge centrifugally. Whether the material leaves the bucket over the outer lip, over the inner lip, or dribbles down the casing is decided by one number: the balance between centrifugal force and gravity at the head.

Take a particle of mass m in a bucket travelling on a circle of radius r at angular velocity ω. It needs a centripetal force mω2r to stay on that circle, and gravity supplies mg. Where the two are equal,

mω2rp = mg  →  rp = gω2 (4.1)

The radius rp is the pole point distance: the distance from the head shaft centre at which gravity exactly balances the centrifugal effect. Writing ω in terms of head shaft speed n in rev/min gives the form used on drawings:

rp = 895n2  m, with n in rev/min (4.2)

The constant is g × 3600/4π2 = 894.6, rounded to 895. Now compare rp with the two radii of the bucket path: the inner radius ri (the belt or chain line at the head pulley) and the outer radius ro = ri + bucket projection.

Table 4.5. Reading the pole point, and what to do when the answer is wrong.
ConditionWhat happensIf that is not what you wanted
rp < ri Centrifugal force dominates over the whole bucket. The load is thrown clear over the outer lip at the head Too fast for a fragile material: reduce the speed, or use a larger head pulley at the same belt speed
rp > ro Gravity dominates. The load rolls out over the inner lip and must be guided — that is continuous discharge Fit continuous buckets so each load lands on the back of the bucket in front, or raise the speed
ri ≤ rp ≤ ro Mixed discharge: part thrown, part poured, trajectory unpredictable. Material hits the casing, falls back down the up leg, and re-circulates Move deliberately to one side or the other. This band is the classic cause of “the elevator only does 70 % of its rated capacity”
Why back-legging wastes power twice

Material that fails to clear the head falls back down the up leg, is picked up again by the next buckets, and is lifted a second time. The tonnage on the belt is higher than the tonnage delivered, so the power is paid twice for the same material and the delivered capacity falls. This is the same re-conveying mechanism as fallback in an inclined screw conveyor (§2.19), and it is diagnosed the same way — by measuring the delivered rate, not the motor current.

The bucket is the part that wears out, the part that decides the capacity, and the part that is bolted through the load-carrying element. It deserves more attention than it usually gets.

Spacing. For centrifugal discharge the buckets must be far enough apart that a thrown load clears the bucket in front. The usual range is 2 to 3 times the projection, and never less than about 1.5 times. Too close and the discharge from one bucket strikes the back of the next, which throws material back into the casing; too far apart and capacity is lost for no reason. Continuous buckets are the opposite case: they are mounted with no gap at all, because the back of each bucket is the discharge chute for the one behind it.

4.7 Belt or Chain?

The buckets hang on either a rubber belt or one or two strands of chain. The choice is made on temperature, abrasiveness, lump size and lift, and it changes the head, the take-up and the maintenance regime.

Table 4.9. Belt against chain.
 Belt (rubber, EP fabric or steel cord)Chain (forged link or roller)
SpeedUp to 3.5 m/s — the fast optionUsually below 1.5 m/s; chain wear rises steeply with speed
TemperatureLimited by the belt: about 80 °C standard, 150 °C with heat-resistant coversTo 400 °C and beyond; the usual choice for clinker, ash and sinter
Lump sizeSmall: a lump trapped between belt and pulley cuts the beltLarge lumps are acceptable; sprockets tolerate them
Noise and shockQuiet, and the belt absorbs shockNoisy, and every link engagement is a small impact
ElongationElastic plus permanent stretch; needs continuous take-up travelWear elongation; links are removed periodically instead
Failure modeTears from a trapped lump, or splice failure; the whole run dropsA single link or pin fails; the run drops the same way, but wear is visible in advance
Typical sectorGrain, food, fertiliser, light mineralsCement, ash, ore, foundry, anything hot or coarse
The bolt holes matter

Bucket bolts pass through the belt, so an elevator belt is a belt with a line of holes punched down its centre, or two lines near the edges. Manufacturers publish the strength of the belt as supplied, and the designer's job is to keep the holes away from the edges, use the fastener the maker specifies, and choose a belt class with margin — see §4.14. Do not make the holes bigger than needed and never re-drill a belt in the field without checking the remaining strength.

4.8 Casing, Head and Boot

The casing is a dust enclosure, a guard, and in a dust explosion it is the pressure vessel. Its design is not sheet-metal detailing.

  • Casing sections are usually 2 to 3 m long, bolted with gaskets, and either single (both legs in one casing) or twin (a separate casing per leg). Twin casings are used for tall machines and where an explosion vent is needed on each leg.
  • Clearance between the bucket lip and the casing wall is typically 25 to 50 mm. Less, and a bucket that shifts will strike the casing — a spark source. More, and the leg is needlessly large, which matters for the explosion volume.
  • The head carries the drive shaft, the head pulley or sprocket, the throat plate and the discharge chute. The position of the throat plate sets where the thrown stream is cut off, and it is adjustable on any decent machine, because the trajectory changes with speed and material.
  • The boot holds the take-up, the inlet, and a cleanout. Boot design decides whether the machine digs or is fed, which is the subject of the next section.
  • Inspection doors at the head, at the boot, and at intervals up the legs. They are for inspection when stopped and isolated, never when running.
Table 4.10. Casing and head clearances, and why each matters.
DimensionTypical valueWhat goes wrong at the wrong value

4.9 Feeding the Elevator: Dig and Flood Feed

How material gets into the bucket is the most underrated decision in the machine, and it is where most capacity complaints start.

Table 4.11. Feed arrangements compared.
ArrangementHow it fillsAdvantagesCosts and risks
Dig (scoop) feedBuckets scoop a bed of material in the boot Self-regulating: the fill adapts to the supply. Simple chute. Tolerates a surging feed Digging resistance adds 10–30 % to the power; degrades fragile material; wears the bucket lip
Flood (loaded) feedMaterial is delivered into the up leg, in the direction of travel Little digging power, gentle on the product, higher fill factor achievable The feed rate must be controlled. Over-feed and the boot fills and plugs; under-feed and capacity falls
CombinationFlood feed with a shallow dig bed as a buffer The common industrial compromise: gentle most of the time, tolerant of surges Needs the inlet geometry to be right; a badly aimed chute gives the worst of both

Three rules follow, and they are worth more than any formula in this chapter. Feed in the direction of bucket travel, never against it. Feed at a rate the machine can take, which means a metering device upstream — often the screw feeder of §2.20. And measure the delivered tonnage before blaming the elevator: an elevator fed at 60 % will deliver 60 %, and nothing inside the casing is wrong.

Designing on 0.9 because the catalogue says the bucket holds 10 L is the single most common sizing error in this machine. A dig-fed elevator on a sluggish powder may run at 0.6, which is two-thirds of the assumed capacity — and no amount of extra motor power will recover it, because the limit is how much material is in the buckets, not how hard they are pulled.

4.11 Capacity Calculation

The capacity of a bucket elevator is a volume per bucket times a number of buckets per second. That is the whole idea; everything else is bookkeeping.

If the buckets are spaced s apart and travel at v, then v/s buckets pass the head each second. Each carries a water-level volume Vb filled to a fraction ψ:

Qv = 3600 Vb ψ vs  m³/h — Vb in m³, v in m/s, s in m (4.3)
Qm = Qv ρ  t/h — with ρ in t/m³ (4.4)

Rearranged, the same expression answers the two questions a designer actually asks. Given a required capacity and a chosen bucket, what spacing is needed? And given a machine that exists, what fill factor is it achieving?

s = 3600 Vb ψ vQv      ψ = Qv s3600 Vb v (4.5)

The second form is a diagnostic tool. Measure the delivered tonnage on a real elevator, convert to volume, and compute ψ. If it comes out at 0.55 when the design assumed 0.75, the fault is in the feed or in build-up inside the buckets — not in the drive, not in the belt, and no amount of speed will fix it cleanly.

Capacity in the CEMA form

The imperial statement of Eq. 4.3 is Q = (bucket capacity in ft³ at water level) × (buckets per foot of belt) × (belt speed in ft/min) × 60, giving ft³/h. It is the same equation with the units folded in, and manufacturers' tables are built from it. Both forms are quoted at water level, so a table that looks generous is usually quoting gross capacity instead — check which.

4.12 Speed Selection

Speed is not free to choose. It has to satisfy three separate conditions at once, and the third is the one people forget.

  1. Capacity. From Eq. 4.3, v and s trade off directly: for a given bucket and fill factor, capacity is proportional to v/s.
  2. Material tolerance. Table 4.14 sets the ceiling. A fragile or abrasive material caps the speed regardless of what capacity would like.
  3. Discharge. The pole point of Eq. 4.2 must land on the right side of the bucket radii for the discharge type chosen. Head diameter and shaft speed are linked to belt speed by
v = π D n60      n = 60 vπ D  D in m, n in rev/min, v in m/s (4.6)

This is the lever that resolves most conflicts. A material that must be lifted at 2 m/s but would be thrown too hard from a small head pulley can be run on a larger head pulley at the same belt speed: the shaft turns more slowly, rp grows, and the discharge moves from violently centrifugal towards gentle. Speed and discharge mode are two different questions with one shared variable.

4.13 Power

Of all the machines in this course, the bucket elevator has the cleanest power calculation, because the useful work is nearly the whole of it. Lifting Qm tonnes per hour through H metres requires

Plift = Qm H g3.6  W — Qm in t/h, H in m (4.7)

which is 2.725 W for every t/h lifted one metre — a number worth memorising, because it lets you check any elevator quotation in your head. A 120 t/h machine lifting 30 m needs 120 × 30 × 2.725 = 9.8 kW of lifting power, so a 30 kW motor on that duty is either allowing for something specific or is simply wrong.

To the lifting power add the digging resistance at the boot and the mechanical losses, then divide by the drive efficiency:

P = Plift ksη  ks = 1.1–1.3 flood fed, 1.2–1.5 dig fed (4.8)
Table 4.15. The scoop and loss factor ks.
ConditionksNote
Flood fed, free-flowing material, short lift1.10Almost pure lifting work
Flood fed, powder, lift to 30 m1.15–1.20Some carry-back and casing friction
Dig fed, free-flowing material1.20–1.30The common industrial case
Dig fed, sluggish or damp material1.30–1.50Digging through a packed bed is expensive
Any elevator with known back-legging—Fix the discharge first; do not buy a bigger motor to lift the same material twice

Drive efficiency η follows the same rules as in §2.15: about 0.94 for a helical reducer, 0.90 for a shaft-mount with a belt primary, and as low as 0.50 for a high-ratio worm gear. Bucket elevators need large reductions — a 1,450 rev/min motor driving a 54 rev/min head shaft is a ratio of 27 — so the temptation to fit a worm box is real, and the efficiency penalty is paid for the life of the machine.

A useful identity

Eq. 4.7 can also be written Plift = Flift v, where Flift is the weight of the material hanging in the up leg (Eq. 4.10). The two forms must agree, and checking that they do catches a surprising number of arithmetic slips: if power from the tonnage and power from the tension disagree, one of the two is wrong.

4.14 Tensions in a Belt Elevator

The belt of a bucket elevator carries the weight of the material in the up leg. The weight of the belt and the empty buckets is the same on both sides and cancels, which is why the elevator is so efficient and why the tension calculation is short.

The material in the up leg at any moment is the tonnage rate times the transit time H/v:

mup = Qm H3.6 v  kg — Qm in t/h (4.9)
Flift = mup g      Fu = Flift + Fdig + Ffric (4.10)

where Fu is the effective circumferential force at the head pulley, the same quantity as in §3.6. The digging force Fdig is taken as 10–30 % of Flift for a dig-fed machine, following Table 4.15, and Ffric covers bearing and seal friction, which is small.

From there the belt analysis is identical to Chapter 3. The slack side must be tight enough that the drive does not slip, which is the Euler–Eytelwein limit:

T2 ≥ Fueμθ − 1      T1 = Fu + T2 (4.11)

With a lagged head pulley, μ ≈ 0.35, and a bucket elevator has a 180° wrap, so eμθ = e0.35π = 3.00 and T2 ≥ Fu/2.00. There is no way to increase the wrap on a vertical machine — no snub pulley geometry helps much — so the take-up tension has to do the whole job. That is why an elevator take-up is not a minor fitting.

The belt is then selected exactly as in §3.9, on tension per unit width at the highest-tension point:

kreq = T1 · SFB  N/mm (4.12)

4.15 Chain Pull in a Chain Elevator

A chain elevator replaces the Euler analysis with something simpler and less forgiving: the chain engages sprocket teeth, so it cannot slip, and the whole of Fu appears as chain pull. With z strands sharing the load,

Fchain = Fuz + F0      Fbreak ≥ Fchain · SF (4.13)

where F0 is a modest pre-tension from the take-up, enough to keep the chain engaged and hanging straight. Three cautions come with a chain machine:

  • Two strands do not share equally. Manufacturing tolerance, wear and an off-centre load put more on one strand than the other. Some designers apply a sharing factor of 0.6 rather than 0.5 per strand, which is a 20 % margin on the pull.
  • Wear elongation is the maintenance clock. A chain that has stretched 2 % no longer matches its sprocket pitch and starts to climb the teeth. Links are removed and the take-up reset — a scheduled job, not a breakdown repair.
  • Impact. Each link entering the sprocket is a small collision, and the tooth count matters: more teeth means a smaller angular step and less impact. Below about 10 teeth the motion is visibly jerky.

4.16 Shafts, Torque and the Starting Case

The head shaft carries the full torque of the drive, and the running torque follows the familiar relation of §2.17:

T = 9550 Pn  N·m — P in kW, n in rev/min (4.14)

Because elevator head shafts turn slowly, that torque is large. A 15 kW drive at 54 rev/min delivers 2,650 N·m — roughly the torque of a small truck engine at peak, applied to a shaft you can put both hands around. Shaft diameter, key, hub and coupling all have to be checked against it, and against the starting case, which is worse:

Tstart = kst Trun  kst = 1.5 normal start, 2.0–2.5 starting a loaded or flooded machine (4.15)

Two situations produce the high end of that range. Starting an elevator whose casing is still full of material — after a power failure, for example — means lifting a fully loaded up leg from rest. And starting with a boot full of settled material means digging through a compacted bed. Both are ordinary events, not abuse, which is why elevators are routinely fitted with soft starters or fluid couplings and why the drive is checked at kst rather than at running torque.

The empty-the-casing question

Before restarting a stopped, loaded elevator, ask whether the machine can start at all. If it cannot, the boot has to be emptied through the cleanout by hand, with the drive isolated. Designing that door in, and the access to reach it, is a five-minute decision on a drawing that saves hours of unsafe improvisation at three in the morning.

4.17 Take-up and the Backstop

The take-up sits in the boot and does two jobs: it provides T2 so the drive does not slip, and it absorbs the stretch of the belt or the wear elongation of the chain.

Table 4.17. Take-up arrangements.
TypePrincipleUse
Screw take-upThreaded rods move the boot shaft downThe standard for short and medium belt elevators. Cheap; needs periodic adjustment
Gravity take-upCounterweighted boot shaft or a weighted frameTall machines and steel cord belts. Tension is constant and automatic
Spring take-upPre-loaded springs on the boot shaftSmall machines, and chain elevators where only pre-tension is needed
Hydraulic or winchPowered, with tension controlVery tall or high-tension machines; allows a different tension for starting

Travel is sized on the same basis as in §3.8. The total belt length is twice the centre distance, so

travel ≈ 0.015 × 2L  fabric belt; the boot shaft moves half of that (4.16)

and 0.3 % of 2L for a steel cord belt, plus 0.3 to 0.5 m for re-splicing. A 30 m elevator therefore needs roughly a metre of belt allowance and half a metre of shaft movement — which is why the boot is as deep as it is.

The backstop is not optional on any elevator that can run backwards under load. Stop a loaded machine and the weight of material in the up leg drives the belt in reverse; the buckets empty themselves into the boot at speed, the boot packs solid, and the drive can be spun backwards fast enough to destroy the reducer. A backstop — a ratchet or a sprag clutch on the head shaft or the reducer — prevents all of that. Check it works. A seized backstop that has quietly failed is discovered on the day the power drops out.

4.18 Dust and Explosion Protection

A bucket elevator handling grain, flour, sugar, starch, coal, sulphur, aluminium powder or most organic solids is a dust explosion hazard, and in the statistics of grain handling the elevator leg is the single most common origin. This is not a footnote to the design; for combustible dusts it is part of the design, and it is regulated.

An explosion needs five things at once — the “explosion pentagon”: combustible dust, dispersion in air, oxygen, confinement, and an ignition source. A bucket elevator supplies the first four by existing. Dust is generated at every dig and every discharge; the moving buckets disperse it; the casing confines it; and the vertical leg is an ideal duct for a flame front to accelerate along. Design therefore attacks the fifth item, and then assumes it will fail anyway.

Table 4.18. The five conditions, and what design does about each.
ConditionPresent in an elevator becauseDesign response
Combustible dustThe material itselfEstablish KSt and minimum ignition energy by test; they set the protection design
DispersionDigging, throwing and impact at the chuteAspirate the head and the boot; feed gently; avoid free fall in the chute
OxygenAir in the casingInerting only in special cases; normally accepted
ConfinementThe casing is a closed ductExplosion relief venting, or suppression, sized to the volume
IgnitionFriction, slipping belt, bucket striking casing, hot bearing, tramp metal, staticInstrumentation and bonding, as below — this is the controllable one
Table 4.19. Ignition sources and their controls.
SourceMechanismControl
Belt slip on the head pulleyFriction heats the belt and the lagging until it smouldersSpeed or motion sensor on the boot shaft, tripping on under-speed; correct T2
Belt or chain misalignmentThe belt rubs the casing; buckets strike steelBelt alignment (drift) switches both sides, high and low
Bearing failureA dry or overloaded bearing reaches ignition temperatureBearing temperature sensors on head and boot shafts, alarm and trip
Tramp metalA steel object strikes the casing or is trapped, making sparksMagnet or metal detector upstream of the inlet
Static dischargeNon-conductive belt and plastic buckets accumulate chargeAnti-static belt and conductive buckets; bond and earth the whole machine
Plugged boot or choked legMaterial packs, the belt slips, everything heatsPlug (choke) switch in the boot, interlocked with the feed
Hot work and smokingExternalPermit system; not a design feature, but part of the same risk register

Protection, not just prevention. Because ignition control can never be complete, a combustible dust elevator is also given a way to survive an explosion. The usual options, in the order they are normally considered:

  • Explosion relief venting — rupture panels in the casing, sized to the casing volume and the dust's KSt value, discharging to a safe place outdoors. The cheapest and most common solution for an outdoor leg.
  • Flameless venting — a vent with a flame arrester, for indoor machines where a discharging fireball is unacceptable.
  • Explosion suppression — pressure detectors trigger a suppressant charge within milliseconds. Used where venting is impossible.
  • Isolation — the leg is isolated from connected equipment so a flame front does not propagate to a silo, which is where a small explosion becomes a large one.
  • Explosion-resistant construction — a casing built to contain the pressure. Rare, because it is heavy and expensive.

A dust hazard analysis is a design input

For a combustible dust, the vent area, the isolation and the instrumentation cannot be chosen from a catalogue. They follow from the dust's own test data — explosion severity KSt, maximum pressure, minimum ignition energy and minimum ignition temperature — and from the casing volume. Establish those numbers at the start of the project, not after the steel is ordered. A design that leaves them until commissioning has already decided to do it badly.

The three classic incidents. First, a dust explosion in the leg, discussed above. Second, someone reaching into the boot or the head to clear a blockage on a machine that was not isolated, and being caught by a bucket. Third, a stored-energy incident: releasing belt tension without securing the take-up, or opening a boot door on a casing still full of material that then runs out. All three are eliminated by the same discipline — isolate, secure the stored energy, and empty before you open — and all three keep happening.

4.20 Worked Design Example

A grain terminal needs to lift wheat from an intake conveyor into a silo distribution conveyor 30 m above. Size the elevator.

Table 4.24. Design data.
MaterialWheat, free flowing, food duty, non-abrasive
Bulk density ρ750 kg/m³ (0.75 t/m³)
Required capacity Qm120 t/h
Lift H30 m, centre to centre
FeedChute into the up leg, with a shallow boot bed — combination feed
ServiceIndoor leg, 16 h/day, wheat dust (combustible)

Step 1 — Volume flow required

Qv = Qm/ρ = 120/0.75 = 160 m³/h.

Qv = 160 m³/h

Step 2 — Discharge type, traction and buckets

Wheat is free flowing and only mildly fragile, the duty is food-grade and the tonnage is moderate: centrifugal discharge on a belt, with HDPE buckets (Tables 4.4, 4.6, 4.9). Plastic buckets are non-sparking, quiet, and will not gouge the belt if one works loose — all three matter in a grain dust leg.

Centrifugal · belt · HDPE buckets

Step 3 — Bucket size and required bucket flow

From Table 4.8 take a 400 × 200 × 200 mm bucket, Vb = 10 L = 0.010 m³, on a 450 mm belt. Table 4.13 gives ψ = 0.75 for free-flowing grain with a combination feed — deliberately not the 0.85 that a perfect flood feed could reach.

Bucket volume flow needed = Qv/ψ = 160/0.75 = 213.3 m³/h, so v/s = 213.3/(3600 × 0.010) = 5.93 buckets/s.

Vb = 10 L, ψ = 0.75, v/s = 5.93 s−1

Step 4 — Speed and spacing

Table 4.14 allows 1.8–2.5 m/s for grain in centrifugal discharge. Take v = 2.25 m/s, which gives s = 2.25/5.93 = 0.379 m. Select the standard 375 mm pitch.

Check the spacing against the bucket: 375/200 = 1.88 times the projection, inside the 1.5–3 range of §4.6. Recomputing the capacity at the selected values, Qv = 3600 × (2.25/0.375) × 0.010 × 0.75 = 162 m³/h, so Qm = 121.5 t/h — 1.3 % above the requirement.

v = 2.25 m/s, s = 375 mm, delivering 121.5 t/h

Step 5 — Head diameter and the pole point

Take a 800 mm head pulley. From Eq. 4.6, n = 60 × 2.25/(π × 0.8) = 53.7 rev/min. From Eq. 4.2, rp = 895/53.7² = 0.310 m.

The bucket path runs from ri = 0.400 m (the belt line) to ro = 0.400 + 0.200 = 0.600 m. Since rp = 0.310 m < ri, the pole point lies inside the whole bucket and the discharge is properly centrifugal (Table 4.5).

D = 800 mm, n = 53.7 rev/min, rp = 0.310 m → centrifugal ✓

Step 6 — Power

Plift = QmHg/3.6 = 120 × 30 × 9.81/3.6 = 9,810 W = 9.81 kW.

With a combination feed take ks = 1.15 (Table 4.15) and a helical reducer at η = 0.92: P = 9.81 × 1.15/0.92 = 12.26 kW.

Select a 15 kW motor

Step 7 — Tensions

Material hanging in the up leg, Eq. 4.9: mup = 120 × 30/(3.6 × 2.25) = 444 kg, so Flift = 444 × 9.81 = 4,360 N.

Check against Step 6: Fliftv = 4,360 × 2.25 = 9,810 W, identical to Plift — the identity of §4.13 holds, so the two chains of arithmetic agree.

Digging allowance 15 %: Fdig = 654 N, so Fu = 5,014 N. With a lagged pulley and 180° wrap, e0.35π = 3.00, so T2 ≥ 5,014/2.00 = 2,503 N and T1 = 5,014 + 2,503 = 7,517 N.

Fu = 5,014 N, T2 = 2,503 N, T1 = 7,517 N

Step 8 — Belt selection

From Eq. 4.12 with SF = 10 on a 450 mm belt: kreq = 7,517 × 10/450 = 167 N/mm.

The next standard elevator belt above that is 250 N/mm. Specify 315 N/mm instead: the extra class covers the bucket bolt holes, allows one re-splice over the life of the belt, and costs very little on a 64 m length.

kreq = 167 N/mm → specify a 315 N/mm belt, 450 mm wide

Step 9 — Torque, starting, take-up and protection

Head shaft torque, Eq. 4.14: T = 9550 × 15/53.7 = 2,667 N·m. At kst = 2.0 for starting a loaded machine that is 5,334 N·m, so specify a soft starter or a fluid coupling and check the shaft, key and coupling at the higher figure.

Take-up travel, Eq. 4.16: total belt length 2L ≈ 64 m, so the allowance is 0.015 × 64 = 0.96 m of belt and 0.48 m of boot shaft movement. Specify 500 mm of screw take-up travel.

Because wheat dust is combustible and the leg is indoors, the machine gets a backstop, a boot motion sensor, belt drift switches, bearing temperature monitoring, a boot plug switch, an upstream magnet, anti-static belt and conductive buckets, and flameless explosion venting sized from the dust's own test data (§4.18).

T = 2,667 N·m running, 5,334 N·m at start; 500 mm take-up travel

4.22 Problems

Before you start

Try the 40-question self-check first. It covers the same ground in multiple-choice form with worked explanations, and it will show you which sections to re-read before you spend time on these.

  1. Safety, first. A maintenance fitter proposes to clear a partly blocked boot by opening the cleanout door while the elevator is running slowly, “so the buckets keep the material moving”. Give three separate reasons why this must not happen, and state what the correct procedure is.
  2. Safety, second. Explain, using the five conditions of Table 4.18, why a grain elevator leg is a more dangerous place than the silo it discharges into — and then explain why an explosion in the leg frequently ends up destroying the silo anyway.
  3. A bucket elevator has 8 L buckets at 300 mm pitch running at 1.6 m/s, handling a material of bulk density 800 kg/m³ at a fill factor of 0.70. Find the volume and mass capacity. [Answer: 107.5 m³/h, 86.0 t/h]
  4. Maize (ρ = 750 kg/m³) is to be lifted at 200 t/h using 14 L buckets at a fill factor of 0.80 and a speed of 2.4 m/s. What bucket spacing is required? [Answer: Qv = 266.7 m³/h, 6.61 buckets/s, s = 0.363 m → select 350 mm and re-check the capacity]
  5. An elevator head shaft turns at 45 rev/min. The head pulley is 1.0 m diameter and the buckets project 250 mm. Find the pole point distance and state the discharge mode. [Answer: rp = 0.442 m; ri = 0.50 m, so rp < ri → centrifugal]
  6. For the same head as Problem 5, what is the highest shaft speed and the corresponding belt speed that would give a purely gravity (continuous) discharge? [Answer: rp > ro = 0.75 m requires n < 34.5 rev/min, i.e. v < 1.81 m/s]
  7. An elevator lifts 90 t/h through 24 m. It is dig fed (ks = 1.2) and the drive efficiency is 0.90. Find the lifting power and the motor power required. [Answer: 5.89 kW lifting, 7.85 kW required → select 9.2 or 11 kW]
  8. For the elevator of Problem 7 running at 1.8 m/s, find the mass of material in the up leg and the resulting lifting force. [Answer: 333 kg, 3,270 N]
  9. Continuing Problems 7 and 8: take the digging resistance as 15 % of the lifting force and the head pulley as lagged with a 180° wrap. Find Fu, T2 and T1. [Answer: 3,760 N, 1,878 N, 5,638 N]
  10. The belt in Problem 9 is 400 mm wide with bolted buckets. Using a safety factor of 10, find the required belt rating, and say what you would actually specify. [Answer: 141 N/mm; specify the next standard class up, with margin for the bolt holes]
  11. The same duty is to be built as a chain elevator with two strands, using a safety factor of 8 on the chain breaking load. What breaking load must each chain have, and what would change if you allowed for unequal load sharing between strands? [Answer: 1,880 N per strand → 15.0 kN minimum; a 0.6 sharing factor raises it to 18.0 kN]
  12. An 11 kW drive turns the head shaft at 40 rev/min. Find the running torque. Then state what torque the shaft, key and coupling should be checked against if the machine can be started with a loaded casing. [Answer: 2,626 N·m running; check at 2.0–2.5 times, i.e. 5,250–6,570 N·m]
  13. An existing elevator with 10 L buckets at 400 mm pitch runs at 2.0 m/s and delivers a measured 75 t/h of a material of density 700 kg/m³. What fill factor is it achieving, and what does that tell you? [Answer: Qv = 107.1 m³/h against a bucket flow of 180 m³/h, so ψ = 0.60 — the machine is under-filled; look at the feed and at build-up inside the buckets, not at the drive]
  14. A 42 m fabric-belt elevator is being designed. How much take-up travel should the boot provide, and how far does the boot shaft move? [Answer: 2L ≈ 88 m, allowance 1.32 m of belt, shaft movement 0.66 m, plus 0.3–0.5 m for re-splicing]
  15. An 8 kW elevator drive turns at 45 rev/min. Find the running torque, and the torque at a starting factor of 2.5. Comment on what that means for the coupling selection. [Answer: 1,698 N·m and 4,245 N·m; the coupling is chosen on the starting figure, not the running one]
  16. A malting plant must lift germinated barley — fragile, damp and slightly sticky — at 40 t/h through 18 m. Choose the discharge type, the traction element, the bucket material and the speed range, and justify each choice from the tables in this chapter.
  17. A cement plant lifts clinker at 180 °C. State what this rules out, what it forces, and three specific provisions you would add that a grain elevator would not need.
  18. An existing elevator delivers 60 t/h at 1.5 m/s with a 700 mm head pulley and 200 mm bucket projection. Production asks for 80 t/h and suggests simply raising the speed to 2.0 m/s. Compute the pole point before and after, and explain what will actually happen to the machine. [Answer: n goes from 40.9 to 54.6 rev/min and rp from 0.534 m to 0.301 m; with ri = 0.35 m and ro = 0.55 m, the machine moves from mixed discharge into proper centrifugal discharge, so the capacity may improve by more than the speed ratio alone — but power rises by 80/60 = 1.33 and the drive must be checked]
  19. Show that lifting one tonne through 30 m costs about 0.082 kWh of useful work, and about 0.109 kWh at the motor shaft for ks = 1.2 and η = 0.9. Compare with a dilute-phase pneumatic system at 1–4 kWh per tonne and comment on when the pneumatic system is nevertheless the right choice.
  20. Design problem. Soybean meal (ρ = 640 kg/m³, free flowing, mildly dusty) must be lifted at 45 t/h through 18 m, flood fed from a screw feeder. Work through steps 1 to 9 of Table 4.3: choose the discharge type and traction, select a bucket from Table 4.8, find the spacing at a sensible speed, verify the pole point for a 600 mm head pulley, then find the power, the tensions, the belt rating and the take-up travel. [Guide: Qv = 70.3 m³/h; with 6 L buckets, ψ = 0.75 and v = 1.8 m/s, s = 0.415 m; n = 57.3 rev/min gives rp = 0.273 m against ri = 0.30 m, so centrifugal; Plift = 2.21 kW and P = 2.94 kW at ks = 1.2, η = 0.9]
