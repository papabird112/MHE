2.1 What a Screw Conveyor Is

A screw conveyor moves bulk material along a trough by rotating a helical flight fixed to a central pipe. The material does not turn with the screw. Friction against the trough wall holds it back from rotating, so the flight face pushes it forward — in exactly the way a nut advances along a bolt when it is prevented from spinning. That single sentence explains most of the machine's behaviour, including why it needs more power as the trough gets rougher, why it stops working if the trough is polished too smooth, and why a vertical screw must run fast enough to press the material against the casing by centrifugal force before it will lift anything at all.

The design is the oldest in continuous use in the whole of materials handling. The water screw attributed to Archimedes dates from about 250 BC, and the machine entered general industrial use a little over a century ago for moving grain and fine coal. It is now one of the most widely used conveyors in the process industries, and by far the most common in agricultural and chemical plant.

Where this chapter sits

Chapter 1 gave the method for choosing between equipment types, and Chapter 3 designs a belt conveyor in the same framework. Table 1.28 puts the screw conveyor's operating envelope at 0.5–10 m distance, 2–300 t/h, lumps to 30 mm and material temperatures to 300 °C. This chapter is what happens after that table has selected a screw: how to size it, how much power it needs, and what will break if you get it wrong.

2.2 Advantages, Limitations and the Design Procedure

What the screw conveyor does well

  • Compact — adapts easily to congested plant, since the trough occupies little more than the swept volume of the screw itself.
  • Meters — volume delivered per revolution is fixed by geometry, so the screw controls the flow of material in operations that depend on accurate batching. This is the basis of the screw feeder in §2.20.
  • Versatile in attitude — horizontal, inclined and vertical installations all work.
  • Mixes — used as a mixer or agitator to blend dry or fluid ingredients, to promote crystallisation or coagulation, or to keep solids in suspension.
  • Seals — can be closed tightly to stop dust or fumes escaping, or to stop dust and moisture getting in.
  • Heats and cools — a jacketed trough carrying hot or cold water or low-pressure steam turns the conveyor into a drier or a cooler.
  • Tolerates hostile material — can be built from whatever alloy resists the corrosion, abrasion or temperature involved.
  • Discharges anywhere — multiple discharge points along the trough, each with its own gate.
And what it does badly
  • Long distances. Torque is transmitted through the pipe and the coupling bolts, and both have hard limits (§2.17). Beyond roughly 10 m per drive, a second conveyor in series is cheaper than a bigger shaft.
  • Fragile material. Everything conveyed is sheared between the flight and the trough. A screw is the wrong machine for material coded Q, degradable.
  • Large lumps. Lump size is limited by the radial clearance between pipe and trough (§2.14), which is a small fraction of the screw diameter.
  • Sticky material. Standard flighting packs solid at the pipe. Ribbon or interrupted flighting exists precisely for this (§2.5).
  • Energy. The material is dragged the whole way. Over a long run a belt will always use less power.

2.4 Screw Geometry: Diameter, Pitch and Hand

Standard sizes

Screw diameters are standardised at 6, 9, 10, 12, 14, 16, 18, 20, 24, 30 and 36 in. (150–900 mm), with larger sizes made to order. Working in whole inches is not affectation: troughs, hangers, covers, end plates, inlets and discharges are all stocked to those diameters, so a screw specified at any other size is a special.

Table 2.4. Pitch classes and what each is for.
PitchDefinitionCapacity factor CF1Used for

ShortS = ⅔ D1.50Inclines above about 20°, and under flood-fed inlets

The capacity factor is the number the required capacity is multiplied by before the screw is selected, so a larger factor means a larger screw. Halving the pitch halves the volume advanced per revolution, so CF1 = 2.00 — the arithmetic is exactly what you would expect.

Right hand and left hand

A screw is right hand or left hand according to the direction the helix wraps, judged by looking at the end of the screw: a right hand helix wraps clockwise away from you, a left hand helix counter-clockwise. Screws are right hand unless something requires otherwise. Hand and rotation together decide which way the material travels, and the pairing is worth committing to memory because getting it wrong on site is embarrassingly common:

Two hands on one shaft

A single screw can carry right hand flighting on one half and left hand on the other. With the two halves meeting in the middle the conveyor collects from both ends to a central discharge; with them starting in the middle it distributes from a central inlet to both ends. The same trick with a short reverse-pitch section immediately before a discharge is used to hold material back and force it out of the opening instead of carrying it past.

2.6 Sectional versus Helicoid; Pipe and Clad Pipe

Flighting is manufactured in one of two ways, and the difference matters more than it first appears.

Table 2.9. The two ways of making a flight.
 HelicoidSectional
How it is madeA flat bar or strip is rolled into a continuous helixIndividual annular plates are formed, then welded end to end
ThicknessTapers from base to tip, because the outer edge is stretchedConstant from base to tip
Maximum thicknessLimited; only light gauges can be rolledAny plate thickness, to 3/4 in. and beyond
CostLowerHigher
UseLight duty, non-abrasive service onlyStandard and heavy duty; anything abrasive
Why the taper matters

Wear is concentrated at the flight tip, which is exactly where a helicoid flight is thinnest. A helicoid flight whose base is 3/16 in. may be only 3/32 in. at the tip — half the metal, in the place that wears first. That is the whole reason helicoid screws are restricted to non-abrasive, light-duty applications. Where the material code carries a 6 or 7 for abrasiveness (§2.10), specify sectional flighting and do not be talked out of it.

The centre pipe

The pipe does three jobs: it carries the flight, it transmits the entire drive torque to the far end of the machine, and it resists the bending caused by the weight of the screw. Standard choices are schedule 40 and schedule 80 pipe in carbon or stainless steel. A third option, clad pipe, is schedule 80 carbon steel pipe surrounded by a schedule 10 stainless sleeve.

Table 2.10. Why clad pipe exists.
PropertyEffect of cladding
Torque capacityThicker combined wall carries more torque than stainless of the same nominal size
Fatigue resistanceCarbon steel resists fatigue far better than stainless. Stainless screws develop stress cracks after a certain number of revolutions; the carbon core postpones that while the sleeve keeps the corrosion resistance
DeflectionStructurally more rigid, so mid-span deflection falls — which lowers fatigue stress again and reduces the risk of the screw touching the trough
PriceOften lower than solid stainless in heavy-duty sizes, since stainless prices have risen
LimitationNot recommended where highly corrosive vapours are present, especially hot — the carbon core is exposed at every cut end

Where the flight meets the pipe there is a corner that can trap product. For most duty a stitch weld is enough. Covers

A cover is not an accessory. The safety rules of §2.23 require the moving screw to be completely enclosed, so the cover is a guard that happens also to keep the weather out.

  • Flanged — the most common, more rigid than flat and seals better.
  • Flat or semi-flanged — works with spring clamps but supports less weight and seals less well. A safety hazard unless properly bolted down, so recommended only in special cases.
  • Hip-roof (ridged) — sheds rain and snow outdoors; flanged both sides for weatherproofing.
  • Shroud — a curved inner cover that follows the screw. Used when a tube trough is wanted but cannot be fitted, and to restrict a flood-fed inlet so material cannot flow over the top of the screw.
  • Dam — a plate that performs the same flood-control job as a shroud. Works well with granular material, less well with anything that clumps.

Clamps are fitted at 600 mm intervals unless specified otherwise: spring clamps for flat and semi-flanged covers, screw clamps for flanged covers, and heavy-duty toggle clamps where a tight seal must be broken often. Gaskets of neoprene, nitrile, silicone, Viton or PTFE are added at trough joints and under covers where dust or liquid must be contained; otherwise silicone caulk at the joints is the standard seal.

2.8 Hangers and Hanger Bearings

A screw longer than one standard section — about 3 m — must be supported somewhere in the middle, and the support can only come from above, because the bottom of the trough is full of material. That is the hanger: a bracket spanning the trough, carrying a plain bearing that the coupling shaft runs in.

The hanger bearing is the weak point of the machine

It is a plain bearing running submerged in the product it is conveying, usually without a seal, frequently without lubrication, and always in the one place nobody can see. It is designed to be sacrificial. Nearly every screw conveyor that fails in service fails here, and almost always because the bearing was allowed to wear through and the shaft then cut into the hanger. The three things that prevent it are choosing the right bearing material, lubricating on schedule, and being able to change the bearing quickly enough that people actually do it.

2.9 Shaft Seals and End Bearings

Wherever a shaft passes through a trough end plate there is a hole, and through that hole the product wants to escape and the atmosphere wants to enter. Screw conveyor shafts run out and deflect far more than a pump shaft, so mechanical seals — which cannot tolerate run-out — are essentially never used. What is used instead:

Reading a seal comparison honestly

The gland seals seal best on paper and are the worst choice on most screw conveyors, because their rated performance assumes an adjustment discipline that plants do not sustain and a shaft run-out that screw conveyors do not deliver. The grease purge seal wins in practice not by sealing better than a packed gland but by still sealing after two years of being ignored. That distinction — between best-case performance and realistic-case performance — is worth carrying into every component selection you make.

End bearings

End bearings sit outside the trough, clear of the product. They come as flange-mounted or pillow block, in ball, roller or spherical roller form, and the choice follows one rule:

  • Drive end — a bearing that takes combined thrust and radial load and does not allow axial float, because the conveying force pushes the screw backwards against it. A roller bearing.
  • Tail end — a bearing that takes radial load and allows expansion, so the screw can grow with temperature without being forced against its own bearings. A ball or spherical bearing.

Pillow block versions on a pedestal end plate move the bearing further from the trough, which is what you want when the product is hot or contaminating. Where thrust exceeds the bearing's capability — long inclined conveyors especially — snap rings are added to the drive shaft.

2.11 Trough Loading and Component Groups

A screw conveyor is never run full. A single characteristic of the material has multiplied the cost of the machine several times over. Both are 30 % loadings, so the volume per revolution is essentially the same. What differs is the speed ceiling: 30 % B caps the screw at roughly half the speed of 30 % A. Choosing the speed within the limit

Equation 2.2 gives the speed a chosen diameter must run at. Two considerations pull that choice in opposite directions:

  • Faster means a smaller, cheaper screw, less trough, fewer hangers and lower capital cost.
  • Slower means less wear (the abrasion score of §2.18 is directly proportional to speed), less product degradation, and less power lost to friction — but a physically bigger machine.

Common practice is to select the smallest diameter whose maximum speed is not exceeded, then check the abrasion score; if it fails, go one size up and repeat. The worked example in §2.21 follows exactly that path.

2.17 Torque and Component Sizing

A screw conveyor is limited in length and size not by power but by torque. Every newton-metre applied at the drive shaft has to pass through the drive shaft, then through the coupling bolts into the first screw's internal collar, then along the pipe, then through the next set of coupling bolts, and so on to the far end. The weakest link in that chain sets the limit for the whole machine.

T = 63,025 × HPN  in·lb      T = 9,550 × Pn  N·m, P in kW (2.15)
Size the torque on the motor, not on the calculation

Use the rated motor power in Eq. 2.15, not the calculated requirement. If the calculation says 3.5 HP you will fit a 5 HP motor, and on the day someone floods the inlet that motor will deliver 5 HP into the screw. Every power-transmitting element should be able to handle the full rated motor power safely. Coupling bolts and bolt pads

Coupling bolts are almost always the weakest element, and two failure modes compete:

  • Shear across the plane between the shaft and the internal collar. Going from a 2-bolt to a 3-bolt coupling raises this limit by 50 %.
  • Bearing of the bolt against the pipe wall, which crushes the hole oval. This is cured by bolt pads — 1/2 in. plates welded over the bolt holes to spread the load. Wall thickness = pipe OD − shaft OD2  — if this is less than the bolt diameter, fit bolt pads (2.16)
Never substitute a hex bolt for a coupling bolt

A coupling bolt has a long unthreaded shank, so that the full shank diameter lies in the shear plane. If the thread of an ordinary hex bolt extends into that plane, the shear area drops to the root diameter: a 3/4 in. hex bolt has only 73 % of the shear strength of a 3/4 in. coupling bolt. This is the single most common field substitution and the single most common cause of a screw conveyor coming apart internally.

Equally, do not over-tighten. Coupling bolts are loaded in shear, not tension. Excessive torque stretches and necks the bolt and permanently weakens it, so the published figures are maximum limits, not targets — tightening below them extends bolt life. Use an accurate torque wrench, and reduce the figure by 20 % for lubricated or plated fasteners, and by 30 % where anti-seize has been used on stainless.

2.18 Deflection, Thermal Expansion and Abrasion

Screw deflection and shaft end angle

A screw is a beam loaded by its own weight, simply supported at its ends. It sags. A standard-length screw rarely sags enough to matter, but a longer section run without an intermediate hanger — which is common on inclined conveyors, where hangers are removed for a different reason (§2.19) — can sag enough for the flights to touch the trough.

δ = W L³76.8 E I  in. at mid-span, horizontal screw (2.17)
Shaft end angle = 180π × 3.2 δL = 183 δL  degrees (2.18)

where W is the total screw weight in lb, L the screw length in inches plus the hanger gap, E = 2.9 × 107 psi for both carbon and stainless steel, and I the moment of inertia of the pipe.

Two checks, not one

A short heavy screw can pass the deflection check and fail the end angle check. Deflection is what makes the flights touch the trough; end angle is the angle the shafts are forced to tilt up at the supports, and it is what destroys bearings and coupling bolts, because the shaft is then trying to rotate inside a bearing it is no longer parallel to. Since deflection goes as L³ and end angle as δ/L, that is L² — the end angle is relatively worse on short spans. Always run both.

The four modifications

  • Eliminate the hangers. A hanger creates a dead zone in the flow, and on an incline that dead zone becomes a dam that the material piles against and slides back from. Removing hangers means longer unsupported screw sections, which brings the deflection and end-angle checks of §2.18 to the front of the design.
  • Tighten the clearance between screw and trough. Essential with granular free-flowing material such as dry refined sugar, which otherwise runs back through the gap faster than it is conveyed.
  • Raise the speed. More forward momentum, less time for material to fall back.
  • Shorten the pitch. This improves the angle between the flight surface and the material, so the flight pushes rather than lets the material slide across it. It is the single most effective change.
  • Enclose with a tube trough or shroud. Both stop product falling back over the top of the screw, which becomes the dominant loss path as speed is increased.
Inclined conveyors need more power for two reasons, not one

The obvious one is lifting the material, Eq. 2.13. The less obvious one, and often the larger, is reconveying the material that falls back. A conveyor at 30° may be moving half as much again as its rated throughput internally, and all of that costs power. This is why the horizontal power equations of §2.15 explicitly exclude inclined duty, and why past experience with the specific product matters so much here. Particle size and shape and moisture content all change the fallback, and none of them appears in any formula.

Vertical screw conveyors

Above about 45° the machine becomes a vertical screw conveyor, and its advantages are distinctive:

  • A very small footprint — it elevates in the space of its own diameter.
  • Very few moving parts, so high reliability.
  • Fully enclosed housing, so contamination potential is very low and fully dust-tight and vapour-tight designs are available.
  • Lifts to about 14 m (45 ft) reliably.
  • Available shaftless for sticky material.

Against that, it must be fed at a controlled rate by a horizontal feed screw, it will not start against a full column of material, and its capacity is sensitive to speed in a way a horizontal screw is not — below a critical speed it simply churns without lifting. The “Vert.” column in Table 2.18 marks which materials will go up at all; the entries marked “?” need testing. Full-scale testing with the actual product, which reputable manufacturers offer, is normal practice here rather than a luxury — and that fact is itself a useful signal about how far the theory can be trusted for this machine.

2.20 Screw Feeders and Live Bottoms

A screw conveyor takes material that is fed to it and moves it. A screw feeder sits directly under a bin or hopper and controls the rate at which material leaves. The inlet section runs flooded — 100 % full — and the screw meters a fixed volume per revolution, so the discharge rate is set by the speed.

Why a feeder needs so much more torque

A feeder must overcome a static condition — material that has been standing and consolidating, possibly overnight — and it must work against the head load, the downward force of the whole column of material in the bin above, which presses the product into the flights and against the trough and generates frictional resistance in proportion. Because feeders also run slowly, and torque is power divided by speed, the torque at the drive shaft is high for a modest motor. The combination of 2½× starting torque and low speed is exactly the condition that shears coupling bolts, so §2.17 matters more on a feeder than on any conveyor.

Drawing evenly: the mass flow problem

A feeder with uniform diameter and uniform pitch does not draw material evenly from under the inlet. The first flight it meets is empty, so it fills there and is full for the rest of the opening: the screw takes everything from the rear of the inlet and nothing from the front. The bin then discharges unevenly, material at the front sits in a dead pocket where it can clump, degrade, absorb moisture or spoil, and in a silo the stagnant column may eventually consolidate into a bridge.

Twin screws and live bottoms

All the single-screw variants are also built as twin screws, which increases capacity and allows a wider inlet and discharge — often the difference between working and not working with material that is not free flowing. Extending that idea, a live bottom is a bank of feeder screws working in tandem that forms the entire floor of a straight-sided bin, drawing material out equally over its full length and width. Live bottoms earn their cost with materials that pack or bridge, which is to say exactly the materials coded X and V in §2.10.

2.21 Worked Example

Problem statement

A feed mill must move cornmeal from the outlet of a hammer mill surge bin to a mixer 30 ft (9.1 m) away, horizontally, at 1,200 ft³/h (34 m³/h). The feed to the conveyor is metered by a rotary valve, so the inlet is not flooded. Plain conveying only — no mixing is required. Design the conveyor: size, speed, motor, components, and confirm it will survive.

The P in the code makes this a food-contact duty, so the hanger bearing is UHMW polyethylene: FDA approved, self-lubricating, good in wet conditions — and Fb = 2.0. From Table 2.26, a 14 in. screw has Fd = 78.

Select a 3 HP (2.2 kW) motor

Notice that the overload factor more than doubles the answer. Without it the requirement would be 1.04 HP and a 1.5 HP motor would look adequate — and would stall the first time the rotary valve surged.

This is the reconciliation promised in §2.16. The two methods look completely different, but once the CEMA overload factor and the metric service factor are recognised as doing the same job, they land in the same place.

What would change the answer

Three plausible changes to the brief would each redesign the machine. If the feed were flooded instead of metered this becomes a feeder (§2.20): 95 % loading, under 30 rpm, and starting torque up to 2½×. 2.23 Problems

Check yourself first

Before working these, try the 40-question self-check. It marks each answer immediately and links back to the section the question came from.

Safety questions — answer these before any of the design problems

  1. Guarding. A maintenance fitter wants to open a trough cover to look at why the conveyor is running noisily. State, in order, every step that must happen before the cover comes off and before it goes back on. Then explain why “the motor was switched off at the starter” is not one of the acceptable answers.
  2. Open inlets. An inlet must be left open because the material is fed by front-end loader. State the two permitted arrangements, and give the maximum grating opening size where the screw is less than 100 mm from possible bodily or clothing contact. [The answers are in the references at §2.24 under ANSI/CEMA B20.1; the grating limit is 1/2 in. × 2 in.]

Material and classification

  1. Reading a code. A supplier quotes a material as 55A45XY. Without being told what it is, state everything you can about the conveyor that will handle it: trough loading, likely component group, flight type, bearing choice, and one thing that will go wrong if you ignore the last two letters.
  2. Building a code. Write the CEMA code for each, and give one design consequence of each character: (a) wood chips, 40 mm, sluggish, non-abrasive, 300 kg/m³, tends to mat together; (b) hydrated lime, 100 mesh, average flow, mildly abrasive, 640 kg/m³, very dusty and aerates; (c) rough rice (paddy) from your own region, measured or estimated.
  3. Two codes for one material. Chapter 1 used the ISO 5048 code (letter first) and this chapter uses the CEMA code (number first). Write both codes for dry sand and explain, in two sentences, which one you would put on a purchase specification for a screw conveyor and why.

Capacity and size

  1. Straight capacity. A grain store must move 900 ft³/h of wheat (47C25N, loading 45 %) with plain full-pitch flighting. Select the smallest standard screw and give its speed. [Answer: 9 in. at 110 rpm]
  2. Capacity factors. A conveyor must deliver 600 ft³/h while mixing, using short pitch, cut flights at 30 % loading, and two 45° reverse-pitch paddles per pitch. Find the equivalent capacity, then select the screw and its speed. [Answers: 1,639 ft³/h; 14 in. at 78.8 rpm]
  3. Metric capacity. A 16 in. screw on 4 in. pipe, full pitch, runs at 60 rpm at 30 % fill, horizontal, on a material of bulk density 750 kg/m³. Find the capacity in m³/h and t/h from Eq. 2.4, then check it against Table 2.21 and state the percentage difference. [Answers: 53.4 m³/h, 40.0 t/h; the table gives 53.0 m³/h, a difference of 0.7 %]
  4. The pipe term. Repeat Problem 8 with the pipe term dropped, so Qv = 47.1 D²Snψ. Then repeat the comparison for a 6 in. screw on 2 3/8 in. pipe. Explain why the error grows as the screw gets smaller, and state the screw diameter below which you would never use the simplified form.
  5. Lump size. A conveyor must handle crushed limestone with a maximum lump of 2½ in., of which about 25 % of the material is lumps between maximum size and half maximum. Find the required radial clearance and the smallest standard screw that provides it. [Answers: 6¼ in.; a 16 in. screw on 4 in. pipe]
  6. Two filters. The conveyor of Problem 10 needs only 400 ft³/h, for which a 9 in. screw at 15 % loading would be more than adequate. State which filter governs, what size you will build, and what the consequence is for the power calculation.

Power and torque

  1. Full power calculation. A 40 ft conveyor moves 1,500 ft³/h of cold soybean meal (40B35, 30 % A loading, 40 lb/ft³, Fm = 0.5), plain full-pitch flighting, oil-impregnated wood hanger bearings, shaft mount drive. Find the screw size, speed, HPf, HPm, Fo and the motor size. [Answers: 14 in. at 72.1 rpm; 0.383; 1.200; 1.743; 3.13 HP, so select 5 HP]
  2. Torque check. For the conveyor of Problem 12, find the torque at the drive shaft using the selected motor, and state whether a 2 7/16 in. shaft in 3 in. schedule 40 pipe with 2-bolt couplings is adequate. Identify the limiting element. [Answer: 4,370 in·lb; the limit is coupling bolt shear at 9,722 in·lb, so yes, by a factor of 2.2]
  3. The overload factor. Plot or tabulate Fo against (HPf + HPm) from 0.2 to 6 HP. Then answer: why is the factor largest where the calculated power is smallest, and what would go wrong if a designer applied Fo = 1.0 to a 0.3 HP conveyor?
  4. Metric power. A 12 m conveyor lifts 25 t/h of cement (λ = 3.8) through 3 m of rise. The screw is 400 mm diameter and the drive efficiency is 0.88. Using Eqs. 2.11–2.14 with ks = 1.25, find the motor power. Then comment on which of the three power terms dominates, and whether that is what you expected.
  5. Worm gear trap. A supplier offers the conveyor of Problem 12 with a worm gear reducer at 75:1 instead of the shaft mount drive. Recalculate the motor power, and state the increase as a percentage and as an annual energy cost at 2,000 h/year and 4 THB/kWh.

Mechanical checks

  1. Deflection. A 20 in. screw weighing 340 lb is mounted on 4 in. schedule 40 pipe over an effective span of 144 in. Find the mid-span deflection and the shaft end angle, and state whether each is acceptable for a standard stainless setup. [Answers: 0.063 in. and 0.080°; both acceptable]
  2. Clad pipe. Repeat Problem 17 with 4 in. schedule 80 clad pipe in place of schedule 40, taking the screw weight as unchanged. State the reduction in deflection as a percentage and explain which two failure modes that reduction postpones.
  3. Thermal expansion. An 18 m stainless steel conveyor at an ambient 30 °C is fed with product that raises it to 150 °C. Find the change in length, and state what must be done about it and at which end. [Answer: about 37 mm]
  4. Abrasion. A 12 in. screw runs at 90 rpm on dry silica sand (95B27) with 3/8 in. flights in a 1/4 in. trough, full pitch. Calculate both abrasion scores. [Answers: 2,262 and 2,700 — both fail] Then propose two different fixes, work out what each does to the numbers, and say which one you would recommend and why.
